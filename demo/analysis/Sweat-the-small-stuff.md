# Deep Analysis — "Sweat the Small Stuff" (Rory Sutherland, TED 2010)

*Demo output of `prompts/01-per-video-analysis.md`, run on the full transcript.*

## 1. Core thesis
Human behaviour changes in **inverse** proportion to the force and expense
applied to changing it: tiny, cheap, psychologically-astute interventions
routinely outperform vast, coercive, expensive ones. Institutions are
structurally blind to this because power and budget are fused in the same
people, who therefore hunt for expensive things to spend money on — so the
highest-ROI "small stuff" goes permanently unowned.

## 2. Argument structure
- **Premise (anomaly):** speed-limit smiley signs beat speed cameras; a £50
  default-change beats a £10m ad campaign (evidence by paired anecdote).
- **Inference:** there is a systematic *disproportionality* — "very small
  changes can have disproportionately huge effects" (10:24).
- **Diagnosis (mechanism):** in institutions "the very person who has the power
  to solve the problem also has a very, very large budget… and once you have a
  very large budget, you look for expensive things to spend it on" (05:18).
- **Reinforcing cause:** the "strategy myth" — boards must believe success flows
  from their big decisions to justify their salaries (08:22) — plus "physics
  envy," the wish that input and output be proportionate and spreadsheet-legible
  (09:23).
- **Conclusion (prescription):** create an owner for the neglected quadrant — a
  "Chief Detail Officer" / "Ministry of Detail" (11:25).
The chain is coherent; its weak joint is that every data point is an anecdote
(§9).

## 3. Named psychological principles
- **The efficiency/effectiveness disproportionality** — Sutherland's signature
  claim that behavioural response is non-linear in spend. Kin to **complexity /
  non-linear systems** (he explicitly analogises to *climatology*, 09:23).
- **Loss aversion / the ostrich effect** — hiding the bank balance so users
  aren't shown "bad news"; making balance opt-in doubles logins (07:21–08:22).
  Origin: Kahneman & Tversky (loss aversion); Galai & Sela (ostrich effect).
- **Defaults / choice architecture** — balance-on-demand vs balance-by-default.
  Origin: Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge*.
- **Social proof** — mothers attending inoculation together raise confidence
  (04:17). Origin: Cialdini.
- **Incentive salience over magnitude** — a kilo of lentils lifts vaccination
  more than the rational stakes warrant (04:17). Source: Banerjee & Duflo.
- **Signalling & the endowed/"forbidden" object** — "Stolen from Virgin
  Atlantic" turns a salt cellar into a memory device by naming the mischievous
  thought (02:16).
- **Peak–end / experience memory** — the memorable trivial detail (lift music,
  cruet) outlives the "strategic" facts (which aircraft, which renovation).
  Origin: Kahneman.

## 6. Contrarian claims
- **Orthodox:** bigger problems need bigger, costlier solutions. **Inversion:**
  cost and effect are decoupled; the cheapest lever is often the strongest.
  **Steelman objection:** survivorship/selection bias — he shows the cheap wins
  that worked, not the thousands that didn't; big spend may buy reliability the
  anecdotes ignore.
- **Orthodox:** strategy (board-level big bets) drives success. **Inversion:**
  strategy is partly a *myth* sustained to justify executive pay; much credit
  lies in tactical detail. **Steelman:** some outcomes genuinely are
  strategy-bound (capital allocation, M&A) where detail can't substitute.

## 7. Practical applications
- **Marketing/branding:** engineer one cheap, *nameable* memory hook (the
  "Stolen from…" move) rather than buying reach; make the customer complicit in
  a small delight.
- **Product/pricing:** audit your **defaults** — the single highest-leverage,
  lowest-cost surface. Hide loss-framed information behind opt-in (banking
  balance). Fix the "user interface" of the whole service, not just software.
- **Persuasion:** reduce force, add the smiley-face feedback loop; convert
  compliance into a social event; use a small tangible token over a rational
  appeal.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest as a *diagnosis of institutional incentives* — the
"power and budget fused in one person" mechanism (05:18) is a genuine,
generalisable insight, and the banking-default and lentils cases are real. It
is weakest **evidentially and in falsifiability**: it is a chain of
hand-picked anecdotes with obvious survivorship bias, and "small things
*sometimes* have huge effects" is nearly unfalsifiable — it never specifies
*which* small things, or how to tell an effective detail from mere trivia in
advance (his own 2×2 admits this quadrant has "no word"). What would change the
picture: a base-rate study of cheap interventions (hit rate, not just the
hits), or a controlled test where detail-optimisation is pitted head-to-head
against strategic spend across many cases.
