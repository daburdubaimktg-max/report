# 13. TEDxNewSt - Rory Sutherland - What is Value?

## 1. Core thesis
Sutherland's central argument is that business — and marketing in particular — has succumbed to a "Stockholm syndrome" of numerical fetishism, forcing every decision to submit to spreadsheets, linear metrics and proportionality when the things humans actually value are non-linear, ordinal and often un-quantifiable. The highest-leverage opportunities ("trim tab moments") come precisely from small, disproportionate interventions on emotional and experiential value that no metric captures — so marketers should be willing, perhaps 20–30% of the time, to "push back" against the tyranny of numbers.

## 2. Argument structure
- **Premise:** Business culture "finds it absolutely impossible to justify or explain or promulgate any business opinion or view or approach unless it has numerical backing" [03:05].
- **Evidence (mostly analogical/anecdotal):** Deal or No Deal and the Goldman Sachs friend [04:06–07:07]; call-centre answer times [07:07–08:07]; the over-representation of finance types on UK boards and of engineers among terrorists [08:07–11:07]; the Austrian School / Von Mises, Goodhart, Cowperthwaite, and the Einstein-attributed sign [11:07–15:10].
- **Inference:** Numbers "dangerously suggest to the statistically less than astute that all things must have some sort of linear relationship" [08:07], and treating "clouds as though they were clocks" (Popper) is the source of the worst mistakes [11:07].
- **Conclusion:** Real value resides in disproportionate, non-numerical effects (framing, ritual, paradigm shifts, hedonic quality); marketing's glory is exactly this disproportionality [17:11 onward], so we should be "unafraid to push back" [24:15].

The reasoning is overwhelmingly argument-by-analogy and anecdote rather than data — ironic and self-aware, since his whole point is that the un-measurable is real. The leap from "some things are non-linear" to "therefore metrics are systematically destroying value" is asserted more than demonstrated; the strongest genuine evidence (call-centre non-linearity, Goodhart's Law) does real work, while the terrorist-engineer digression is entertaining color that only loosely supports the thesis.

## 3. Named psychological principles
- **Diminishing marginal utility (hedonics):** "money has a diminishing marginal utility and actually a 20,000 Caravan is not twice as good as a £10,000 caravan" [06:06]. He recasts the "logarithmic" contestant behaviour as *more* rational than the banker's linear model. Roots in Bernoulli / behavioural economics (Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory implicit).
- **Loss aversion / anticipated regret:** the failure to "account for regret the pain of long-term regret" — refuse £20,000 and "you do spend the next 30 years of your life feeling like a [expletive]" [05:06–06:06]. Regret theory (Loomes & Sugden); loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky).
- **Non-linearity / the illusion of linearity:** the call-centre example [07:07–08:07] and the explicit warning that numbers imply linear relationships where none exist. This is his signature "psycho-logic."
- **Framing effect:** organ-donation opt-in vs opt-out wording change [17:11]; video-conferencing framed as "the poor man's air travel rather than the rich man's phone call" [17:11–18:11]. Kahneman & Tversky; also Thaler/choice architecture (default effects on organ donation, cf. Johnson & Goldstein).
- **Choice architecture / defaults:** the organ-donation default is a textbook Thaler-Sunstein nudge, invoked implicitly [17:11].
- **Goodhart's Law:** "any metric that becomes a Target loses its value as a metric" [13:09] — measurement gaming (Charles Goodhart), illustrated with hospital waiting lists, airline push-back, cancelled trains.
- **Clouds vs clocks:** Popper's distinction, "the worst mistakes... is treating things that are like clouds as though they were clocks" [11:07] — a metaphor for probabilistic vs deterministic systems.
- **Ordinal vs cardinal preference:** Von Mises' claim that "human preference is ordinal and not scalar" and cannot be depicted numerically [12:08–13:09] — Austrian praxeology.
- **Paradigm shift:** borrowed from Kuhn (he mis-cites "Dena Meadows" [21:12], apparently meaning the concept), applied to Hi-Fi→iPod and Diner→McDonald's [18:11–20:11].
- **Ritual / signalling (implicit):** the Panama Limited whistle broadcast as a "pure demonstration of the reliability of the Train" [01:04] — a costly, public signal of reliability; branded ritual as long-term brand-building.

## 4. Case studies & examples
- **The Panama Limited train & WSM radio ("We Shield Millions")** [00:04–02:05]: a punctual train whistled past the Nashville radio tower, which broadcast the whistle so "the American South used to set its watch by this train." Evidence FOR branded ritual and the "long now" of brand-building. Genuinely apt as a signalling anecdote, though it opens the talk almost as an unplanned aside.
- **Disney trains / "When You Wish Upon a Star"** [02:05–03:05]: his attempt to replicate the ritual failed because Disney didn't own the song rights. Charming, self-deprecating; mostly rhetorical setup.
- **Deal or No Deal / the Goldman Sachs friend** [04:06–07:07]: the "hyper rational" player assumes linear money-value and ignores regret; real contestants are actually more rational given diminishing utility and regret. Strong support for the diminishing-utility and regret points.
- **Call-centre answer times** [07:07–08:07]: firms spent heavily cutting rings from eight to three assuming linearity; in reality up to ~10 rings was fine, over 10 people got angry. The talk's cleanest, most data-like evidence for non-linearity.
- **Engineers among terrorists** [09:07–11:07]: engineering degrees over-represented among fundamentalist terrorists (Gambetta & Hertog's research, unnamed but referenced as "two sociologists"); a "mindset... that disdains ambiguity and compromise." Evidence FOR the claim that engineering/numerical mindsets seek absolutism — but the link to marketing is loose; it's largely provocative color.
- **Goodhart illustrations** [13:09–14:09]: hospital waiting lists, airline departure measured "from push back," cancelled trains ("a train that doesn't exist can't actually be late"). Strong, concrete support for metric-gaming.
- **Cowperthwaite / Hong Kong** [14:09–15:10]: forbade the collection of economic statistics lest they "encourage people to interfere." Evidence FOR the idea that measurement invites meddling; doing rhetorical work at the extreme end.
- **Train punctuality vs tables** [16:10–17:11]: "the whole point of rail travel is that there's a table"; spending to arrive two minutes earlier "while... some other guy is ripping out the tables" destroys real value. Core illustration of measurable-crowding-out-valuable.
- **Video conferencing / telepresence** [17:11–18:11]: failed because framed as cheap air-travel substitute (like pager to mobile, margarine to butter) rather than "the rich man's phone call." Framing/positioning evidence.
- **Hi-Fi → iPod** [19:11–20:11]: paradigm shifted from sound-reproduction perfection to portability in "about 3 months." Evidence for sudden frame-of-reference change.
- **American Diner → McDonald's** [19:11–20:11]: value redefined from choice to speed. Same point.
- **Mobile-phone miniaturisation & 3G licences** [23:14–24:15]: phones shrunk past usability; operators "paid 16 billion for a 3G license" they could never recoup once broadband went unmetered — "pissing billions of pounds up the wall." Evidence for engineers pursuing a metric "to the point of absurdity."
- **Eurotunnel** [24:15–end]: £6bn to save ~30 minutes; a marketing director's customers said "that was the worst thing you ever did... it's too damn fast"; half a billion spent on hedonic improvements would have delivered more. The capstone example of misallocated, metric-driven spend.
- **Buckminster Fuller / trim tab** [20:11–21:12]: Fuller called himself "trim tab" — the small part "where the smallest movement has the biggest effect on the direction of the craft." The organizing metaphor for disproportionate leverage.
- **Jeremy Clarkson / Top Gear** [21:12–23:14]: reviews cars whose "numerical metrics... are very good or very bad but his emotional reaction... is entirely different" ("worse at everything... but I prefer it"). Evidence that much of value "cannot be explained perfectly in numerical terms."

## 5. Quotable lines
- "What marketers have developed is a kind of stockhome syndrome where we've been so beaten up by procurement and finance and other people that we've started to take on the worst qualities of our abusers" [04:06].
- "Most of the worst mistakes that are made in life is treating things that are like clouds as though they were clocks" [11:07] (paraphrasing Popper).
- "Any metric that becomes a Target loses its value as a metric" [13:09] (Goodhart's Law).
- "Not everything that counts can be counted and not everything that can be counted counts" [15:10] (the sign attributed to Einstein).
- "The whole point of rail travel is that there's a table... the idea that all the effort and money is being spent making the thing arrive 2 minutes earlier while at the same time some other guy is ripping out the tables is exactly the kind of problem where atrocious metrics lead to a massive destruction of real economic value" [16:10–17:11].

## 6. Contrarian claims
- **Orthodox view:** Rational Deal-or-No-Deal play means taking the expected-value-maximising choice; real contestants are irrational. **Inversion:** given diminishing utility and regret, the "irrational" contestants are actually the sensible ones [05:06–07:07]. **Steelman objection:** diminishing utility and regret can themselves be modelled numerically (utility functions, regret theory) — so this is an argument for *better* metrics, not against metrics.
- **Orthodox view:** More precise metrics and shorter response/arrival times are straightforwardly better. **Inversion:** beyond a threshold there's "no gain whatsoever," and pursuing the metric destroys value elsewhere [08:07, 16:10]. **Steelman:** the answer is threshold-aware measurement, not abandoning measurement.
- **Orthodox view:** Finance/engineering discipline should dominate the board. **Inversion:** that mindset "disdains ambiguity," treats clouds as clocks, and pursues metrics "to the point of absurdity" [11:07, 23:14]. **Steelman:** he cherry-picks catastrophic metric failures while ignoring the vast, invisible value of reliable measurement (his own airplane-wheel-nut caveat [03:05] concedes this).
- **Orthodox view:** Value should be proportionate to input. **Inversion:** marketing's glory is disproportionality — "one very very small intervention can have absolutely massive effects" [21:12]. **Steelman:** disproportionate effects cut both ways and are largely unpredictable ex ante (he admits the "price... of unpredictability"), which makes them hard to deliberately manage or defend to a board.

## 7. Practical applications
- **(a) Marketing/branding:** Invest in the "long now" — durable branded ritual (the train whistle, When You Wish Upon a Star) over obsessing about "breadth and reach" [00:04]. Reframe products: sell telepresence as "the rich man's phone call," not the poor man's air travel [18:11]. Watch for paradigm shifts in the "current measure of value in your business" — treat that as "a vital marketing function" [20:11].
- **(b) Product & pricing:** Expect non-linear utility — a £20k caravan is not twice a £10k one [06:06]; don't over-invest chasing metrics past their threshold (call-centre rings, phone miniaturisation, arrival times). Beware Goodhart gaming when you set targets [13:09]. Allocate large budgets to hedonic experience (tables, journey pleasure) rather than the easily-measured axis (speed) — the Eurotunnel counterfactual [24:15].
- **(c) Everyday persuasion:** Tiny wording/default changes produce huge behavioural swings (organ-donation framing) [17:11] — hunt for "trim tab" interventions where a small move steers the whole craft [21:12]. Respect regret and emotional reaction (the Clarkson point) as legitimate decision inputs [21:12].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks
- **Psycho-logic / non-linearity of human value** — the spine of *Alchemy*; recurs wherever Sutherland attacks "economic rationality."
- **Framing & positioning** ("poor man's air travel" vs "rich man's phone call") — a recurring reframing move across talks.
- **Costly signalling / branded ritual** (the train whistle) — links to his signalling-theory material (Zahavi/Veblen) in other talks.
- **The engineer/finance mindset vs marketing** — recurring antagonist; "clouds vs clocks" (Popper) as a repeated frame.
- **Diminishing marginal utility & regret** — connects to prospect-theory threads elsewhere.
- **Trim tab / disproportionate leverage (Buckminster Fuller)** — a signature metaphor likely reused.
- **Goodhart's Law / measurement corrupts** — recurs in his critiques of KPI-driven management.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where it has near-data — the call-centre non-linearity and Goodhart's Law give concrete, generalisable teeth to the claim that metrics both mislead (via assumed linearity) and corrupt (via gaming). It is weakest, and effectively unfalsifiable, in its central positive claim: that huge value "resides" in un-measurable qualities. Because he defines the valuable precisely as that which resists measurement ("there isn't a metric for a beautiful jug" [23:14]), no metric could ever refute him, and successful "trim tab" interventions are only knowable after the fact, which quietly undercuts their usefulness as management advice. He also stacks the deck with disaster stories (3G, Eurotunnel) while conceding the enormous value of measurement (wheel-nuts) only in passing, and his own honest caveat — that he'd be "very content for the business to become more scientific" and pushes back only "20% of the time maybe 30%" [15:10, 24:15] — reveals the position is really a plea for balance dressed as heresy. What would change the picture: prospective, pre-registered evidence that "hedonic" spend (tables, framing, ritual) reliably out-returns metric-driven spend, or a systematic accounting of how often small interventions actually produce the promised disproportionate effects versus quietly failing.
