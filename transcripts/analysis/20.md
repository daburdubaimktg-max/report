# 20. Rory Sutherland's 2026 Predictions

## 1. Core thesis
Sutherland argues that businesses have collapsed into two defensive modes — cost reduction and regulatory paranoia — and that AI will initially be sold as another instrument of cost-cutting and headcount reduction, destroying uncounted value in the process. The correct response, licensed by the fact that value is subjective (Austrian school), is to treat AI as a discovery mechanism that improves customer experience and reinvents the process rather than merely automating the existing one. The deepest strategic move for marketers is to stop selling *what they do* and start selling *how they think*.

## 2. Argument structure
- **Premise:** Most businesses operate in only two modes — cost reduction and regulatory paranoia — while only marketing, innovation, and R&D genuinely add value (invoking Drucker) [00:05], [04:09].
- **Evidence (by anecdote/analogy):** The "doorman fallacy" [01:05], self-checkout tills producing shoplifting [02:05]/[03:07], Royal Mail's postie [08:13], the online travel agent's 0.5% vs 30% conversion [11:19], and the electric motor's phased adoption [13:20]–[15:21]. These are almost entirely illustrative anecdotes and historical analogies, not datasets — the one hard number cluster is the IPA award winners [13:20] and the conversion figures [11:19].
- **Inference:** Because credit for cost savings is claimable but destroyed value is never charged back (an asymmetric accountability structure), efficiency logic will win by default — it "fits really well on a spreadsheet" [05:11] — even when it is value-destructive.
- **Conclusion:** AI adoption will pass through three phases — (1) "same but cheaper," (2) "same but better," (3) genuine reinvention (ad agencies producing content proactively; Cannes as trade fair) [12:19]–[17:25]. Marketers should reframe their value as a way of thinking, not a deliverable [17:25]–[20:28].
- **Does the leap hold?** The reasoning is largely by analogy and asymmetry argument. The electric-motor analogy is genuinely load-bearing and historically robust; the claim that AI *must* follow the same three phases is plausible but asserted, not demonstrated.

## 3. Named psychological principles
- **Opportunity cost / asymmetric accountability** — his central mechanism: credit for cost cuts is claimable, but "you're never held accountable for any destruction of value or indeed an opportunity cost" [02:05]. Not a labelled bias but a governance-incentive critique; his own signature framing ("doorman fallacy").
- **Quantification bias / McNamara fallacy** — "what you can measure quickly suddenly becomes more important than vastly more important things that are difficult to measure at all, like trust" [09:15]. He attributes the phrase in-room to "Tom." Closely tied to Goodhart's law (metrics becoming targets) and Charles Goodhart / Robert McNamara.
- **Subjective theory of value (Austrian school)** — marketing as value *creation*, not cost; explicitly credited to Ludwig von Mises (the cook vs. floor-sweeper in a restaurant) [04:09]–[05:11].
- **Utility-maximisation / stable transitive preferences (Chicago school)** — the rival view that "people already know what they want... a stable and transitive utility function" [05:11], which he rejects.
- **The human/social element of service** — customer satisfaction is "disproportionately the human element of any exchange" [07:12]; halo effect of the likeable postie over objective service quality [08:13].
- **"Different is better than better"** — his own heuristic, a psycho-logic point about distinctiveness over incremental optimisation [07:12], [12:19].
- **Focusing illusion / metric fixation** — Concorde optimised speed/time/distance while ignoring how metrics "translate to human perception" [19:26]–[20:28]; echoes Kahneman's focusing illusion.
- **The 180° flip** — credited to Mark Ritson: looking at a problem "from an angle and a standpoint that nobody else in the business looks at it at" [17:25]–[18:25].
- **Darwinian discovery** — business as "a discovery mechanism... in an almost Darwinian way" exploring adjacent value [06:12]; evolutionary-epistemology framing.

## 4. Case studies & examples
- **The doorman fallacy** [01:05]: replace a hotel doorman with an automatic door, claim the salary savings, ignore the destroyed functions (hailing taxis, recognising regulars, security, status). Evidence FOR asymmetric accountability. Genuinely supports the claim — it is a clean illustration, though it is a constructed parable rather than a documented case.
- **Self-checkout tills** [02:05]–[03:07]: finance notices customers can do the work; option becomes obligation; shoplifting explodes (avocados scanned as onions — "selling more onions than they were actually buying"). Strong support for the value-destruction-goes-uncounted thesis; also a real, widely-reported phenomenon.
- **Gain-share agreements** [01:05]–[02:05]: consultancies take a cut of identified savings but bear no liability for consequent value destruction. Direct evidence for the incentive asymmetry.
- **Royal Mail / Alex Batchelor** [08:13]: heavy spend on on-time-delivery reliability had no effect on brand perception; brand liking tracked whether people liked their postie. Evidence that the human element dominates measurable operational metrics. Presented as a tested theory, so it carries more weight than the parables.
- **Online travel agent conversion** [11:19]: web visitors convert ~0.5%, phone callers ~30%. Evidence against forcing customers down the lowest-cost channel. He concedes it's "not a like-for-like comparison," which is an honest caveat that partly blunts the rhetorical force.
- **IPA Effectiveness Awards 2024** [13:20]: four of five winners were family-owned (McCain, Waitrose, Yorkshire Tea, Specsavers), the fifth Guinness. Evidence that freedom from quarterly reporting enables real marketing. Suggestive correlation, not causal proof.
- **The electric motor** [13:20]–[15:21]: steam-engine-to-single-big-motor gave trivial gains; only rethinking the whole process (many small distributed motors) unlocked the technology. The key analogy for how AI should be adopted. Historically accurate and the strongest structural argument in the talk.
- **Fabergé egg vs. Wedgwood** [15:21]–[16:23]: bespoke-on-demand production vs. mass production that then goes and finds customers — the origin of modern marketing, and a template for AI-era agencies producing content proactively.
- **Cannes as trade fair** [16:23]–[17:25]: prediction that agencies will produce speculative content and find buyers (as social-media stars already do for brands like KFC), turning Cannes from retrospective into trade fair.
- **Concorde** [18:25]–[20:28]: engineered by engineers with no marketers; brilliant east-to-west ("going faster than the Earth rotates"), a "crock" on the return leg because of human/circadian scheduling. Evidence that ignoring human perception in favour of convenient metrics produces dumb decisions.

- "Don't sell what you do. Sell how you think." [17:25]
- "Unless you have a marketer in a room, you're at risk of doing things which are seriously dumb." [20:28]

## 6. Contrarian claims
- **Orthodoxy:** Efficiency and cost reduction are the reliable path to profit. **Inversion:** Cost reduction is an asymmetric con — savings are credited, destroyed value is never charged back — so "efficiency competition" is often value destruction in disguise [02:05], [06:12]. **Steelman objection:** In genuinely commoditised markets with price-elastic customers, efficiency *is* the durable advantage; his best examples (hotels, service brands) are experience goods and may not generalise to bulk B2B or commodities.
- **Orthodoxy:** Marketing is a cost centre to be justified to finance. **Inversion:** Marketing is value creation, co-equal with manufacturing (von Mises) [04:09]. **Steelman:** Without measurable accountability, "marketing is value creation" is unfalsifiable and can rationalise unlimited spend — the very critique finance levels at it.
- **Orthodoxy:** Drive customers to the lowest-cost channel to maximise online conversion. **Inversion:** More channels and human contact mean more sales; hiding the phone number is "absolute insanity" [11:19]. **Steelman:** At scale, human channels may be genuinely unaffordable per unit; the 0.5% vs 30% figure ignores volume and selection effects (callers are pre-qualified high-intent buyers), which he half-concedes.
- **Orthodoxy:** Public-company discipline (quarterly reporting) drives performance. **Inversion:** It renders serious, long-horizon marketing "more or less impossible"; family-owned firms win because they can work across timescales [12:19]–[13:20]. **Steelman:** Survivorship/selection bias — we notice the family firms that won awards, not the many that failed without market discipline.

## 7. Practical applications
- **(a) Marketing/branding:** Reframe the marketing function's pitch from deliverables to a way of thinking — "sell how you think" [17:25]; the market for that is "literally 100 times bigger" than for what you do [18:25]. Deploy the 180° flip: bring the consumer's standpoint into rooms full of engineers/finance to prevent "seriously dumb" decisions [20:28]. Bet on "different is better than better" as an escape from the coming sea of homogeneous AI content [07:12].
- **(b) Product & pricing:** Treat AI as a reinvention tool (phase 3), not a headcount tool (phase 1) — rethink the whole process the way factories rebuilt around distributed electric motors [13:20]–[15:21]. Protect and reward the human element: the best call-centre agents may be worth six figures but go unpaid for it because they "can't prove their value" [10:18]–[11:19]. Keep human channels open; don't hide the phone number [11:19]. Explore proactive/speculative production of marketing content and find buyers after [16:23]–[17:25].
- **(c) Everyday persuasion:** Recognise the human element trumps objective service quality — likeability drives evaluation (the postie, the house sale that collapses on personal dislike [10:18]). Beware optimising convenient metrics (speed, cost, time) that ignore how they land in human perception — the Concorde return-leg lesson [19:26].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks
- **Doorman fallacy / value destruction uncounted** — a signature Sutherland construct likely recurring across the series and central to *Alchemy*'s critique of efficiency.
- **Subjective theory of value / Austrian vs Chicago economics** — his economic bedrock; von Mises, Hayek likely to reappear.
- **"Different is better than better"** — repeated distinctiveness heuristic, links to Byron Sharp / brand distinctiveness.
- **Quantification bias / unmeasurable value (trust, retention)** — recurring attack on the McNamara fallacy and Goodhart's law.
- **The human element in service** — persistent theme; expect more postie/call-centre/estate-agent style anecdotes.
- **Reinvention analogies (electric motor, Fabergé vs Wedgwood)** — his stock of technology-adoption parables.
- **Focusing illusion / convenient metrics (Concorde)** — links to Kahneman across the series.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest as an incentive critique: the asymmetry whereby cost savings are credited and destroyed value is never charged back is genuinely illuminating, and the electric-motor analogy gives the AI-adoption thesis real structural depth. It is weakest at the point of measurement — Sutherland's whole edifice rests on value that is "difficult to measure at all," which makes his claims hard to falsify and vulnerable to the exact finance-department retort he mocks ("justify all this money"). His evidence is overwhelmingly anecdote and analogy; the two quantified cases (0.5% vs 30% conversion; four-of-five family-owned award winners) are confounded by selection effects that he only partly acknowledges. What would change the picture: longitudinal data showing that firms which preserved human channels or resisted AI-driven headcount cuts actually outperformed efficiency-maximisers on lifetime value or retention — the "slow" metrics he insists matter but does not here measure.
