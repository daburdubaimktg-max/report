# 35. Rory Sutherland – Are We Now Too Impatient to Be Intelligent? | Nudgestock 2024

## 1. Core thesis
Sutherland argues that modern institutions, technologies and optimisation models are relentlessly biased toward speed and efficiency — treating time saved as an unquestioned good — when in fact human value is often located precisely in slowness, effort and inefficiency. Because mathematical and algorithmic models cannot represent the ambiguity of human psychology (where "the opposite of a good idea can be another good idea"), they systematically strip out subjective experience, and we have become "too impatient to be intelligent." The corrective is to consciously decide which things deserve to be accelerated and which deserve to be deliberately slowed down.

## 2. Argument structure
- **Premise:** Human perception of time is subjective and non-linear, not the regular, physicist's quantity that models assume. **Evidence:** The pace-ometer / "Pomer" showing that accelerating from 10→30 mph saves 10 minutes over 10 miles while 70→80 mph saves only a minute [02:07–03:07]. **Inference:** Framing the *same* physical fact differently reverses behaviour, so time is psychological, not merely physical. This leap holds — it is a clean reframing demonstration.
- **Premise:** Organisations prefer closed, single-answer problems because they let decision-makers avoid blame. **Evidence:** The HS2 engineering brief (speed/capacity) vs. the imagined Disney brief ("make the journey so enjoyable people feel stupid going by car") [06:10–07:12]; the Transport for London modellers who forbid the finding that people enjoy commuting because it "f***s up our whole model" [11:15–12:15]. **Inference:** "Business people, governments, politicians aren't looking to solve problems, they're looking to win arguments," and optimisation is a rhetorical device for dodging accountability. This is largely anecdote-driven but internally consistent.
- **Premise:** Technologies begin as options and harden into obligations, at which point universal adoption makes everyone worse off. **Evidence:** Email made instantaneous; parking apps (4 in 5 motorists want rid of them); phone-filming at concerts ruining it for performers [09:13–17:16]. **Inference:** Individually rational adoption produces collectively bad outcomes (a coordination failure). Supported by analogy rather than data, but the mechanism (switching costs, social norms) is plausible.
- **Conclusion:** Because value frequently lives in inefficiency, and AI/optimisation defaults to acceleration, we must consciously identify what to slow down — otherwise we "get things terribly, terribly wrong" [30:27]. The argument moves mostly by analogy and anecdote; its weakest joints are the un-sourced or anonymised claims (TfL modeller, commuting economists), its strongest is the reframing logic that is self-demonstrating.

## 3. Named psychological principles
- **Framing effect** (Kahneman & Tversky): the pace-ometer reframes identical time/distance data to reverse the perceived value of speed [02:07–04:08]. Sutherland uses it as a life-saving safety argument and a rail-ticketing sales argument.
- **Costly signalling** (Zahavi; Spence's market signalling): "there's a costly signaling system at work and I think our drive to efficiency in advertising is actually self-defeating" [26:24]. The effort/expense of a medium correlates with its persuasiveness — hence the handwritten house letter [26:24–27:24].
- **Uncertainty reduction / anxiety vs. duration:** the Uber map "doesn't change how long you wait… it changes the quality of the wait… by reducing uncertainty" [08:12]. Distinguishes emotional state (disquiet, anxiety) from the numerical variable (time).
- **Choice architecture / Nudge** (Thaler & Sunstein): the handwritten letter framed explicitly as "an extraordinary kind of nudge" [27:24]; email defaults and server-check intervals as design levers [09:13–10:14].
- **System optimisation vs. local optimisation** (W. Edwards Deming): "if you want to optimize the system you have to sub-optimize the parts" [14:15] — applied to video conferencing and email coordination.
- **Switching costs** (from algorithm/computing theory, applied to human attention): rapid task-switching destroys efficiency; Paul Graham's "maker's schedule / manager's schedule" and Dickens' "the mere consciousness of an engagement will sometimes worry a whole day" [10:14–15:15].
- **Cost disease** (William Baumol, named "William Ball's model of cost disease"): time-dependent services rise in cost while manufactured goods fall — TV as impulse buy vs. agonising over childcare; Agatha Christie's three servants but no car [24:23–25:23].
- **Social acceleration / alienation** (Hartmut Rosa, "harm Rosa"): acceleration since the Industrial Revolution leaves us permanently unsatisfied and "under-optimised" [17:16–18:17].
- **Option-to-obligation ratchet** (Sutherland's own framing, akin to a collective-action / arms-race dynamic): "it starts as an option, it then becomes an obligation" [16:16].
- **Unaccountability / algorithmic blame-avoidance** (Dan Davies, *The Unaccountability Machine*; "computer says no"): reducing decisions to formulae removes ambiguity and therefore removes blame [12:15–13:15].
- **Psycho-logic / union of opposites** (Sutherland's signature concept): "the opposite of a good idea can be another good idea" [20:19]; New York fast speech vs. Southern slow speech both signalling politeness [21:19]; Cialdini's ("Robert Shalini") scarcity working via both "everybody has one" and "few have one" [21:19–22:20].
- **SCARF model** (David Rock — Status, Certainty, Autonomy, Relatedness, Fairness): acceleration + automation + unaccountability attack status, certainty, autonomy, relatedness and fairness [19:18–20:19].
- **Disutility-of-travel assumption** (transport economics): the modelling axiom that all travel time is disutility, which Sutherland attacks with the "chronological buffer" commuting evidence [11:15–12:15].

## 4. Case studies & examples
- **The pace-ometer / "Pomer"** [02:07–04:08]: minutes-per-10-miles gauge. Evidence FOR the subjectivity/non-linearity of time and a genuine safety point (high speed buys almost no time). Genuinely supports the claim; also used to argue high-speed rail is "dumb" — that extension is more rhetorical.
- **Rail ticketing (Waterloo to Exeter St Davids via Salisbury)** [05:10–06:10]: cheaper, more scenic first-class route hidden because algorithms assume you want to save time. Evidence that optimisation defaults erase preference. Supports the claim.
- **Google Maps refusing scenic routes as "biased"** [06:10]: evidence that "objectivity" is itself a value-laden default. Rhetorical but apt.
- **HS2 vs. a hypothetical Disney brief** [06:10–07:12]: engineers optimise speed; Disney would optimise enjoyment ("so enjoyable people feel stupid going by car"). Doing rhetorical work — it is a thought experiment, not a case — but illustrates the closed- vs. open-question point well.
- **Uber's map** [08:12]: reduces uncertainty, not wait time. Strong, concrete evidence for the anxiety-vs-duration distinction.
- **Guinness ("good things come to those who wait")** [08:12–09:13]: slow pour turned into brand virtue; bartenders hated it. Genuinely supports "turn a weakness into a strength."
- **Email made instantaneous** [09:13–10:14]: burden shifted to every recipient; a "productivity disaster." Analogy/assertion, no data, but mechanistically coherent.
- **Transport for London commuting research** [11:15–12:15]: anonymised expert finds people enjoy commuting (men more; a "standby mode," coarse fishing as "staring with equipment"); people live further from work than optimal to create a decompression buffer; modellers suppress it. Central anecdote — persuasive but unverifiable and single-sourced.
- **Video conferencing / Zoom** [13:15–15:15]: normalisation, not invention, is the change; he claims to have advised Zoom's marketing director in 2019 to build a "Thank Zoom It's Friday" day, with a dark joke about needing "a small pandemic." Illustrates the coordination-failure point.
- **Parking apps (4 in 5 motorists want them gone)** [16:16]: option → obligation, imposed even where there's no signal. Supports the ratchet claim.
- **Concert filming** [16:16–17:16]: ruins it most for performers ("sucked into people's telephones"). Supports the social-norm/coordination argument.
- **Turner's *Rain, Steam and Speed*** as Hartmut Rosa's cover [18:17]: the hare being run down by the train — a visual for acceleration's cost. Rhetorical illustration.
- **19th-century transcontinental railway to California** [15:15–16:16]: people imagined more leisure; instead speed became the expected standard (you could no longer pretend you'd gone by ship). Historical evidence for the option-to-obligation ratchet — well-chosen.
- **Political slogans (MAGA, Take Back Control, "Deutschland aber normal")** [18:17–19:18]: all retrospective/past-referential, signalling disquiet with the pace of change. Cultural evidence, doing interpretive/rhetorical work.
- **Starbucks slowing baristas to two drinks at a time** [23:22–24:23]: batch/parallel processing destroyed the "handmade" experience; deliberate slowdown restored value. Strong, concrete counter-example to "faster is better."
- **Farmer's markets / Saturday shopping** [22:20–23:22]: people deliberately shop in the most inefficient way for pleasure. Supports value-in-the-journey.
- **Hotel check-in (fully automated phone unlock vs. being escorted and made tea)** [22:20]: two opposite yet both-great experiences. Illustrates union-of-opposites.
- **Baumol cost disease (TV impulse buy vs. childcare; Agatha Christie's servants but no car)** [24:23–25:23]: economy-wide evidence that time-dependent services resist compression.
- **Quooker-style boiling-water "cooker tap"** [25:23–26:24]: a 30-minute Zoom sales demo needed to sell an £800 kettle — evidence that some products must be "sold slow," and that advertising is mis-defining audiences as those reachable via cheap, fast media.
- **Handwritten house-purchase letter (family with triplets, Chesterfield)** [26:24–27:24]: handwritten letters to ~15–25 owners of un-listed houses; ~8 responded, ~5 invited them, 4 offers. Evidence for costly signalling / effort-as-persuasion. Compelling anecdote though unverifiable.
- **Student essays / AI essays** [27:24–29:25]: the finished essay is worthless; the value is the effort of producing it. AI "shortcuts from request to delivery" and bypasses the valuable part. Core analogy for the AI-advertising warning.
- **Jeremy Bullmore's advice** [29:25]: a B2B client with no media budget should still make a campaign it never runs, because the process of making it is more valuable than the ad. Supports "value is in the process."

## 5. Quotable lines
- "Business people, governments, politicians aren't looking to solve problems, they're looking to win arguments." [07:12]
- "The opposite of a good idea can be another good idea." [20:19]
- "In New York you speak quickly because you respect the value of the other person's time… in the South you speak slowly because you want to respect the person by showing how much of your own time you're prepared to give to them." [21:19]
- "There are things where the value is precisely in the inefficiency, in the time spent, in the pain endured, in the effort you have to invest — that's actually where the value comes from." [29:25]
- "What does slow AI look like? We've automatically assumed that the way we interact with it is instantaneous. Are we sure that's right?" [29:25–30:27]

## 6. Contrarian claims
- **Orthodoxy:** Faster is always better; time saved is pure gain. **Inversion:** For much of life, slowness *is* the value (Guinness, Starbucks, farmer's markets, hotel tea, essays). **Steelman objection:** Revealed preference generally shows people paying premiums for speed (express shipping, high-speed trains that do sell out); Sutherland cherry-picks cases where slowness happens to signal, and offers no rule for when the inversion applies beyond intuition.
- **Orthodoxy:** High-speed rail is progress. **Inversion:** It's "kind of dumb" because meaningful time savings only occur at plane-competitive distances; the right brief was Disney's "make it enjoyable." **Steelman:** Capacity relief, reliability and network effects — not just individual journey time — often justify HSR; the "Disney brief" is untested and may not scale economically.
- **Orthodoxy:** Optimisation models are objective and therefore trustworthy. **Inversion:** They are blame-avoidance machines that delete human psychology precisely because it's ambiguous. **Steelman:** Models are removed *because* subjective preference is hard to measure reliably and easy to game; discipline and comparability are real benefits, not just cowardice.
- **Orthodoxy:** Advertising should maximise efficiency/reach per pound. **Inversion:** Efficiency in advertising is self-defeating because persuasion is a costly signal; effort itself communicates. **Steelman:** Costly signalling explains some brand effects but plenty of cheap, high-frequency advertising demonstrably works (performance/direct response); the claim risks being unfalsifiable.
- **Orthodoxy:** AI's value is speed of output. **Inversion:** AI may destroy value by bypassing the effortful process that was the real product (essays, ad development). **Steelman:** For many outputs the artifact genuinely is the value (a correct answer, a working translation); "the process was the point" applies to learning and strategy, not to all knowledge work.

## 7. Practical applications
**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Treat effort and expense as messages: a costly, slow, high-craft communication (handwritten letters, long demos, un-run campaigns) can out-persuade cheap high-reach media [26:24–29:25].
- Don't define your target audience as merely "those reachable cheaply and fast" — that silently narrows you to the low-signal segment [26:24].
- Rebrand time: convert a slow process into a virtue ("good things come to those who wait") rather than apologising for it [08:12].
- Use the process of making advertising as a strategy tool even absent a media budget (Bullmore) [29:25].

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Optimise for the emotional variable (uncertainty, anxiety, trust), not just the numerical one (duration) — the Uber-map lesson [08:12].
- Deliberately slow experiences where craft-perception drives value (Starbucks two-drink rule); design "escorted-and-made-tea" premium tiers alongside automated self-service [22:20–24:23].
- Sell high-consideration products slowly (30-minute demos) and price for the time the persuasion actually requires [25:23–26:24].
- Design for the option-to-obligation trap: technologies that are pleasant when optional become resented when universal — build in opt-outs and coordination (email buffers, server-check intervals, "Zoom Fridays") [09:13–16:16].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Reframe the same fact to change behaviour (pace-ometer safety point) [02:07–04:08].
- Match the signal to cultural context: speed or slowness can both convey respect [21:19].
- Invest visible effort in a request when the stakes are high; the medium's weight carries the message (handwritten letters) [26:24–27:24].
- Batch communications and resist instantaneity to protect your own "maker's schedule" [10:14].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks
- **Psycho-logic / union of opposites** — recurs throughout *Alchemy*; "the opposite of a good idea can be another good idea" is a core Sutherland motif.
- **Costly signalling as the engine of persuasion and branding** — a central *Alchemy* thesis (expensive, inefficient signals are credible).
- **The tyranny of optimisation / "the map is not the territory"** — models deleting subjective value; likely echoed wherever he critiques economics and spreadsheets.
- **Reframing over changing reality** — subjective value creation (Uber, Guinness) as cheaper than physical improvement.
- **Focusing illusion / mismeasurement** — treating time (or any single metric) as the whole story; a likely Goodhart's-law adjacency.
- **Behaviour-as-social-norm / coordination failures** — option-to-obligation ratchet; connects to network-effect and collective-action discussions elsewhere.
- **Anti-efficiency in advertising / "the process is the product"** — recurring critique of data-driven, programmatic marketing.
- **Slow AI** — a forward-looking hook likely to recur as AI features across later talks.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where it is self-demonstrating — the pace-ometer reframing and the Uber-map distinction between anxiety and duration are genuinely illuminating and hard to dispute — and where it identifies a real institutional pathology: optimisation models that suppress inconvenient human truths to preserve their own integrity and to launder accountability (the TfL and email cases capture something true about organisational life). It is weakest, and edges toward unfalsifiability, in the sweeping claim that "value is in the inefficiency": Sutherland offers no criterion for *when* slowness adds value versus when it's simply friction, so the thesis can retrofit any outcome (self-checkout is bad here, good there), and much of the support is charming but single-sourced or anonymised anecdote (the Chesterfield letter, the secret TfL expert, the Zoom conversation) rather than data. His strongest empirical anchor — Baumol's cost disease — actually undercuts the romanticism by showing slowness is often just expensiveness we can't escape. What would change the picture: controlled evidence on when effort/slowness measurably increases willingness-to-pay or persuasion (isolating signalling from mere quality), revealed-preference data on how often consumers actually choose slow when price-equalised, and a testable rule distinguishing value-adding inefficiency from waste. Absent that, the talk is best read as a powerful corrective heuristic — "ask which things deserve to be slowed down" — rather than a proven law.
