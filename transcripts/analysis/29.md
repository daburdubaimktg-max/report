# 29. The psychology of digital marketing. Rory Sutherland, Ogilvy

## 1. Core thesis
Human beings do not perceive objective reality; they act on subjective, context-dependent perception, so the only thing that matters in behaviour is the *perception* of a thing, not the thing itself. Because economics and finance assume people are logical, proportional, context-free optimisers, they systematically misunderstand value — which means marketers can create enormous economic value cheaply by changing perception rather than reality, and should stop imitating the finance department by pretending to be equally "logical."

## 2. Argument structure
Sutherland builds a layered case, mostly by analogy and anecdote rather than data:

- **Premise 1 (framing):** There are two ways to create economic value — find what people want and deliver it, or work out what you can deliver and make people want it — and both are equally valuable [03:08]. Finance treats marketing as an inefficiency because their model assumes "perfect information and perfect trust," conditions that "never exist" and that our brains never evolved to operate in [08:09–09:10].
- **Premise 2 (psychophysics / evidence):** We literally don't see reality. The TV screen is a "hack" specific to human trichromatic vision; yellow and purple photons producing those sensations don't exist [19:13–24:14]. Evolution "doesn't give a shit about accuracy, it only cares about fitness" [18:13]. Therefore perception ≠ reality is a *biological fact*, not a marketing metaphor.
- **Inference:** If perception is a leaky, relative, constructed representation, then the same reality can produce different emotions and the same emotion can be produced by different realities [42:43]. So you can improve subjective experience "by an order of magnitude" more cheaply than you can improve objective reality [05:09].
- **Evidence cluster:** A long chain of examples (trains, elevators, queues, call centres, Uber, brands) each illustrating that psychological intervention beats engineering on cost-effectiveness.
- **Second inference (signalling):** Value is also relational and temporal — repeated-game dynamics and costly signalling explain brands, advertising, engagement rings and why cheap digital advertising may be structurally weak [1:19–1:27].
- **Conclusion:** "Be weirder." Test things that don't make sense, because logical moves are what competitors also make, so the returns lie in the counter-intuitive [1:27:08].

The reasoning is a chain of vivid analogies; the psychophysics section is the one piece grounded in hard science (colour vision, McGurk effect), and it does real load-bearing work — it converts a marketing opinion ("perception matters") into a claim about the nervous system. The leap that mostly holds is perception≠reality; the leap that is looser is the inference from "perception is malleable" to "psychological problems are easy" [07:09], which he asserts rather than demonstrates.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Behavioral economics vs neoclassical rationality** (Thaler, Kahneman; Munger/Buffett quip) — his framing device: economics that "observes what people actually do" vs "elaborate mathematical formulae for what they should do in a completely imaginary parallel universe" [00:03].
- **Psychophysics** (Fechner/Weber lineage, though unnamed) — perception is non-linear and relative to reality; central organising concept [17:13].
- **Trichromatic colour vision / perception as construction** — yellow and purple as brain inventions; "purple... essentially represents not green" [23:14]. Species-specific perception.
- **McGurk effect** (McGurk & MacDonald) — vision overrides audio ("ba" heard as "fa"), demonstrating cross-modal, constructed perception [29:17–30:17].
- **Sensory leakage / crossmodal correspondence** — "human perception is leaky"; heavier bottle → better wine, round chocolate → sweeter, clean car → smoother drive [26:14–27:14].
- **Anchoring & relative pricing** — Nespresso pod judged against Starbucks not Nescafé; Rolls-Royce shown at yacht shows after Lear jets [32:17–33:17].
- **Placebo effect** — "the most powerful single thing in medicine... most scientists spend their time trying to minimize it" [16:13]; branded/expensive painkillers work better [26:14].
- **Framing / loss aversion** (Kahneman & Tversky) — "50% extra free" beats "33% off"; bonus vs bribe indistinguishable economically but not psychologically [27:14].
- **Uncertainty aversion / operational transparency** — dot-matrix displays, Uber's approaching-car map, "we'll text you 45 minutes before" [46:45–47:46].
- **Social proof** (Cialdini) — "Most people choose B" tripling conversion [51:50]; best-selling car in Jamaica heuristic [1:15:01].
- **Satisficing** (Herbert Simon) — the darts analogy: aim for 19 not triple-20 to minimise catastrophic variance [1:09:58]; also invoked for Dyson ("more satisficing") [56:53].
- **Substitution / attribute substitution** (Kahneman) — buying a used car, you substitute "do I trust the seller?" for the unanswerable engineering question [52:51].
- **Affect-then-rationalise / confabulation** — emotions come first, reasons are "reached off the shelf" post-rationalisations; the mother-in-law Trip Advisor review [43:43–45:45].
- **Variance/risk minimisation & "what's the worst that can happen?"** — the cat-and-cucumber, penalty kicks down the middle, McDonald's low-average-low-variance; brands as insurance and variance reduction [1:10–1:17].
- **Costly signalling theory** (Zahavi; Veblen for conspicuous consumption; Spence for market signalling) — engagement rings, wedding invitations on card, TV advertising, FedEx letters; "something costly to say is sincere" [1:21–1:27].
- **Repeated games / continuation probability** (game theory, evolutionary biology; iterated prisoner's dilemma à la Axelrod) — "long-term selfishness is almost indistinguishable from cooperation" [1:20:06].
- **Jack-of-all-trades heuristic** — single-purpose things seem better at their one job; Google's single search bar [49:48].
- **Ludwig von Mises / Austrian subjective theory of value** — explicitly named; value is entirely subjective, so Austrians are "well disposed to marketing" [35:18].
- **Skin in the game** (Taleb, unnamed) — the racehorse-owner backing his horse; advertising spend as reputational risk [1:25:07].
- **Ergodicity** — named but deliberately skipped ("I won't explain, it's complicated") as one of the false assumptions of economic logic [1:02:56].
- **Commutativity fallacy in aggregated data** — "1×7 = 7×1 only in maths"; ten people standing 10% of the time ≠ one person standing 100% [13:13–15:13].

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **High-speed rail (HS1 / HS2):** £6bn to shave 40 minutes when Wi-Fi, or supermodels handing out Château Pétrus, would improve the journey more cheaply [05:09–06:09]. Evidence for: subjective improvement beats objective. Rhetorical flourish, but the Wi-Fi point is genuinely persuasive.
- **The Manchester train app (£200k vs £60–70bn HS2):** let early-arriving advance-ticket holders board earlier trains for £5, cutting journey time and increasing yield-managed capacity; EasyJet already does this [10:11–13:13]. Strong, concrete, genuinely supportive of the "change perspective not engineering" claim.
- **Train overcrowding:** give worst-affected season-ticket holders first-class upgrades rather than lengthening platforms [15:13–16:13]. Supports the anti-commutativity point.
- **Amazon overvalued:** "good way for eight people to buy one thing" but not "one person to buy eight things"; Walmart more efficient [14:13]. This is doing rhetorical/illustrative work for the aggregation-loses-information point rather than a rigorous valuation claim.
- **Otis elevators / mirrors:** floor-to-ceiling mirrors killed "too slow" complaints for near-zero cost vs millions for new gear [47:46–49:48]. The signature parable; strong support.
- **Philips Air Fryer:** great product hobbled by a bad name ("air sauteuse" would have sold), and shelved next to deep-fat fryers [36:18–37:18]. Supports "market it badly and it's worthless."
- **Ed Sheeran peep show (Hamish & Andy):** world-class product, 2h23m of no takers until scarcity bias ("gets busy later") closed it [37:18–43:43]. Vivid support that presentation dominates product; also demoing scarcity as a live tactic.
- **Broadband/engineer appointment slots:** people don't hate the lack of a one-hour window, they hate *uncertainty* and "house arrest"; "we'll text 45 mins before" solves it cheaply [43:43–47:46]. Strong.
- **London Underground dot-matrix displays:** biggest satisfaction gain, not faster trains — 9 known minutes beats 4 uncertain minutes [47:46].
- **Uber:** "not a disruptive technology" (phone taxis since 1903); the win is watching the car approach [55:52–56:53].
- **Google's single search bar:** jack-of-all-trades heuristic beat the portal model [48:47–49:48].
- **Dyson bagless:** the visible dirt is satisfying (progress), not functionally superior; "more psychology than engineering" [56:53].
- **Call-centre "Most people choose B":** conversion up 2.5–3× from social proof [51:50].
- **Coloured caps (Diet Coke red cap):** colour changes taste; regular drinkers found it "almost undrinkable" [52:51–53:51].
- **EasyJet bus reframe:** "the bus takes you all the way to passport control" turned a grievance into a perk [53:51–54:51].
- **Red Bull:** the most successful anti-Coke — tiny can, expensive, "tastes disgusting" — because weird taste signals psychotropic potency; Diet Coke must taste worse than Coke so the sacrifice is believed [58:54–1:00:54].
- **Low-cost airlines / Ryanair:** advertise what you *don't* get to make the low price believable — "destigmatise a low price" [1:01:55–1:03:56].
- **Nestlé identical-price machines / cheaper-and-better paradox:** if a choice "doesn't make sense," people buy neither [1:00:54–1:02:56].
- **Restaurant wine list choice architecture:** wine glasses pre-set, "wine list" not "drinks list," one list handed to the head of the table forcing "red or white?" [1:03:56–1:05:57]. The most detailed choice-architecture dissection.
- **Airline price-comparison trap:** when the only visible variable is price, everyone (even the rich) picks the cheapest; the 7am flight false economy; sell "least crowded flight" or "brand-new 787" instead [1:05:57–1:08:00].
- **Ferrari factory collection:** framing "pay £500 to tour Maranello and drive it home" makes customers pay to collect their own car [1:08:00].
- **Selfridges wrapping / Five Guys extra fries / engagement rings / wedding invitations / FedEx letter:** costly, post-purchase or upfront signals of commitment [1:21–1:27].
- **BlaBlaCar:** brilliant except for the 2% "worst that can happen" (the film *The Hitcher*); variance dominates [1:18:02].
- **Diageo cocktail menus:** no man orders a cocktail without a picture of the glass, for fear of a 0.01% chance it arrives in a pineapple [1:17:02].
- **Penalty kicks:** shooting down the middle scores more but you "look much stupider if you fail" — reputational variance [1:13:00].

## 5. Quotable lines

- "Evolution doesn't give a shit about accuracy, it only cares about fitness. And if evolution can distort reality and gain a survival advantage, that's exactly what it does." [18:13]
- "The bastard with the spreadsheet always wins... finance people trust savings, but they don't trust an increase in revenue." [13:13]
- "The main activity of business is ass-covering with value creation as a small secondary sideline." [57:54]
- "Long-term selfishness is almost indistinguishable from cooperation. Short-term selfishness is almost indistinguishable from conflict." [1:20:06]
- "Marketing... is the science of knowing what logic is wrong about... Be weirder and don't let the finance people insist that everything you do is logical, because everybody else is doing logical things. That's exactly what they'd expect you to do." [58:54 / 1:27:08]

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Orthodoxy:** to increase sales, cut the price. **Inversion:** you can increase demand by *raising* the price (expensive painkillers, tonic wines, Red Bull) [27:14]. **Steelman objection:** this works only for goods where price signals quality/efficacy under genuine uncertainty; for commodities and price-elastic staples it fails, and he concedes discounting "works more often."
- **Orthodoxy:** a better *and* cheaper product is an easy win. **Inversion:** people buy neither because the choice "doesn't make sense" [1:00:54]. **Steelman:** the effect likely shrinks with familiar categories, reviews, and information; it may be an artefact of a specific low-information context.
- **Orthodoxy:** advertising is messaging (content is king), and cheap/targeted digital is therefore superior. **Inversion:** advertising is *signalling*; its cost is the message, so cheap digital may be structurally weaker at building confidence [1:23:06–1:27:08]. **Steelman:** performance/digital advertising's measurable ROI is real; costly-signalling value is hard to falsify and conveniently defends expensive TV budgets Ogilvy profits from — a potential conflict of interest.
- **Orthodoxy:** reduce objective wait times, journey times, delivery windows. **Inversion:** manage uncertainty and attention instead (mirrors, displays, texts) [47:46]. **Steelman:** psychological fixes have ceilings; beyond a point objective performance genuinely matters, and he under-weights that.
- **Orthodoxy:** aggregate data reveals truth; big data is objective. **Inversion:** aggregation destroys information and perception intermediates every data point ("15 pounds ≠ 15 pounds reduced from 29.99") [23:14–26:14]. **Steelman:** correct in principle, but he offers no method for when aggregation is safe vs misleading.
- **Orthodoxy:** rational, defensible decisions are good management. **Inversion:** "no one ever gets fired for coming up with a logical answer... logic is mainly used in business to provide an alibi" [17:13] — logic is often ass-covering, not truth-seeking.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Sell brands as *variance reduction / insurance*, not as objective superiority [1:16:02].
- Make advertising *visibly costly* (TV, FedEx, physical mail) to signal confidence; don't assume cheap digital delivers the same trust [1:23–1:27].
- Reframe grievances into perks (EasyJet bus); mix a bad thing with a good thing so attention diverts to the upside [53:51].
- Use scarcity and social proof deliberately ("most people choose B," "gets busy later") [43:43, 51:50].
- Name products for the buyer's self-image ("air sauteuse" not "air fryer") and place them where the target buyer looks [36:18].

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Price relative to the buyer's reference frame; choose the comparison set (yacht shows, Starbucks anchor) [32:17].
- To make a low price believable, advertise what you *don't* get — destigmatise, don't just discount [1:02:56].
- Avoid "better and cheaper" incoherence; make the trade-off legible so the choice "makes sense" [1:00:54].
- Add believable sacrifice (weird taste, visible cost) when claiming special potency [1:00:54].
- Give comparison-shopped categories non-price dimensions to choose on ("least crowded flight," "brand-new aircraft," "kids go half price") [1:05:57].
- Design for progress and reduced regret (visible dirt, mirrors, dot-matrix, approaching-car map) [47:46–56:53].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Reduce *uncertainty*, not just waiting/effort ("I'll text 45 minutes before") [46:45].
- Front-load costly signals of commitment in relationships you want to endure (engagement ring, wedding-card stock, post-purchase generosity) [1:21–1:23].
- Behave as if every interaction is a repeated game — cooperation and unasked-for value pay off when continuation probability is high [1:19–1:21].
- Distrust people's stated reasons; treat complaints as sincere emotions with confabulated justifications, and fix the real (often unspoken) driver [43:43].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **Perception ≠ reality / psycho-logic** — the spine of *Alchemy*; recurs wherever he discusses subjective value.
- **"The opposite of a good idea can be a good idea"** — the "be weirder," non-commutative-logic motif.
- **Costly signalling as the unifying theory of advertising, brands, and courtship** — likely a recurring set-piece (engagement rings, peacocks, wedding invitations).
- **Variance minimisation / "what's the worst that can happen?"** — the evolutionary-risk lens (McDonald's, brands-as-insurance, penalty kicks) that reappears across talks.
- **Attack on economics' assumptions** (context-free, ergodic, individualistic, proportional) — his standing critique of neoclassical rationality; pairs with von Mises/Austrian subjectivism.
- **Operational transparency / uncertainty** (Uber, Underground, engineer slots) — a recurring "reduce anxiety not time" theme.
- **The finance-vs-marketing power struggle / "ass-covering"** — organisational-behaviour thread likely echoed elsewhere.
- **Repeated games and continuation probability** — Trip Advisor / tourist-trap dynamics; ties to trust and reputation across the series.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where it rests on hard perceptual science — trichromacy, the McGurk effect, colour-context illusions — because these convert an aesthetic claim ("perception matters") into a neurological fact that even a finance director must concede, and the cheap-intervention case studies (elevator mirrors, the £200k train app, dot-matrix displays, appointment-window uncertainty) are genuinely compelling and testable. It is weakest where it becomes unfalsifiable and self-serving: the costly-signalling defence of expensive TV advertising is convenient for an ad agency, and "cheap digital can't build confidence" is asserted more than shown, ignoring performance advertising's measurable returns. His method — a rapid montage of memorable anecdotes — is persuasive but selection-biased: he narrates the wins and rarely the psychological interventions that flopped, and he offers no framework for *when* perception beats reality versus when objective performance is decisive (a plane must actually be safe, not merely feel safe). The sweeping claim that "psychological problems are easy" [07:09] is the most over-reaching. What would change the picture: controlled A/B or field-experimental data quantifying the durability and effect sizes of these hacks (many placebo-type effects fade once known or habituated), plus honest reporting of failures and of ceilings where objective improvement re-dominates.
