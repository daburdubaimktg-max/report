# 36. The Hidden Psychology Fueling WOKE Madness – Rory Sutherland

> **Note on scope.** This is a short (~8 minute) conversational clip, not a structured talk. The clickbait title ("WOKE Madness") oversells it: the actual content is a loose two-way conversation between Sutherland and his host (who references being Jewish, David Baddiel, and interviewing gender-critical/trans figures — likely Andrew Gold of *Heretics*). Sutherland's substantive contributions cluster around three ideas: the **purity spiral** as a signalling dynamic, the value of **perspective-taking / measured opinion**, and how **incentive structures shape identity over time** (the journalist thought-experiment). The analysis below focuses on Sutherland's positions and flags where the host is speaking.

## 1. Core thesis
Online moral extremism is not primarily about belief but about **signalling group allegiance**: because a moderate position signals nothing, the only reliable way to prove loyalty to a tribe is to adopt a more-extreme-than-average stance, which produces a self-reinforcing "purity spiral" of ever-escalating sensitivity. The corrective — genuinely understanding other people's points of view — is undervalued precisely because it earns no social reward and even feels transgressive to express.

## 2. Argument structure
The clip is discursive rather than tightly argued, but Sutherland's central chain runs:

- **Premise:** Humans have a biological imperative toward group membership and them-and-us bias ("it's certainly a biological imperative to um membership of groups... and to them and us bias as well" [02:02]).
- **Premise:** Within a group, allegiance must be *signalled*, and a merely average position doesn't distinguish you as committed.
- **Inference:** Therefore the "only way satisfactor[il]y to signal that allegiance is to take a more extreme than average position" [01:01] — signalling costs must exceed the baseline to be credible.
- **Evidence (by analogy):** Football fans who "paint their faces" [01:01]; the "footballization of politics" [01:01].
- **Conclusion:** This creates "a feedback loop or a spiral where people become more and more princess and the P about potential violations" [01:01] — the purity spiral, with hyper-sensitivity as the demonstration of commitment.

A **second, supporting argument** concerns the counterfactual — why moderation is scarce. Sutherland offers a personal experiment (posting measured opinions on Twitter [02:02]) and reports two failure modes: measured takes "get you absolutely no [traction]" and "it feels really weird writing it" [02:02]. The Rob Manuel "@f*sh*le / non-opinion" example [03:02] supplies the mechanism: balanced views survive only anonymously because openly holding them risks cancellation.

A **third thread** (the Hugh Edwards / Dan Wooten and Piers Morgan riff, largely host-led at [08:04]) illustrates how sustained incentive gradients sculpt two would-be journalists into "two totally different people" — an argument that character is downstream of reinforcement, not fixed.

**Where the leaps hold / don't:** The signalling-drives-extremism inference is well-supported *in principle* by costly-signalling theory, but Sutherland argues almost entirely by analogy (football, road rage, journalism) and self-report, with no data. The football analogy is genuinely illuminating; the road-rage story [05:03] is really about misattribution/perspective-taking and is only loosely tied to the signalling thesis.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Signalling / costly signalling.** The engine of the whole argument: allegiance is proven by taking a costlier (more extreme) position than the group average. Sutherland doesn't name Zahavi or Spence, but the logic is pure handicap-principle costly signalling (Amotz Zahavi) crossed with Michael Spence's market-signalling — a signal is only credible if it's expensive to fake, so escalation is structurally required.
- **The purity spiral.** Sutherland explicitly disclaims coining it ("that's not my phrase I wish it were" [01:01]) and attributes it loosely to Chris Williamson and Rob Henderson. Coined by Gavin Haynes; conceptually a runaway signalling feedback loop.
- **In-group/out-group bias (them-and-us bias).** Named directly [02:02] as a "biological imperative" — Tajfel's social identity theory / Henri Tajfel's minimal-group paradigm, though Sutherland doesn't cite him.
- **Tribalism / group selection framing.** "trying to root out whoever's not quite on your level and get them out the tribe" [02:02] — the host's line, but Sutherland endorses the biological framing.
- **Preference falsification.** The "non-opinion" phenomenon [03:02] — opinions people hold but are "too embarrassed to actually share," expressible only anonymously — is a textbook instance of Timur Kuran's *preference falsification*, where public conformity masks private dissent and distorts the perceived distribution of opinion.
- **Perspective-taking / theory of mind.** "understanding things from other people's point of view... is just a really important life skill" [04:02], illustrated by the road-rage misattribution [05:03].
- **Fundamental attribution error / misattribution.** The road-rage story [05:03]: he read the beep as hostile ("blocking the road") when it was cautionary ("letting him know he was there") — a hostile-intent attribution bias.
- **Incentives shape identity (reinforcement / behavioural conditioning).** The Hugh Edwards vs Dan Wooten thought experiment [08:04]: identical aspirants diverge into "two totally different people" because one is rewarded for gravitas ("we'll give you a knighthood") and the other for scoops ("more money"). This is operant conditioning applied to character formation — arguably Sutherland's implicit answer to the fundamental attribution error at the level of biography.
- **Chekhov's gun (narrative principle, invoked explicitly).** [06:04] — not psychology proper, but a cognitive/narrative expectation principle; introduced re: Larry David.

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **Andrew Doyle deplatforming row (host-supplied).** Doyle took his Twitter down after backlash for interviewing Debbie Hatton, a trans woman; gender-critical hardliners objected to his "platforming" her [00:00]. **Evidence for:** the purity spiral in action — allies attacking allies over insufficient purity. Genuinely supports the thesis: the punishment comes from one's own side for a moderate act.
- **Football fans who paint their faces [01:01].** Sutherland's anchor analogy for costly, extreme allegiance-signalling; he frames football as "purely anthropological and bizarre." **Evidence for:** signalling as tribal display. Rhetorically effective and conceptually apt — the analogy carries real explanatory weight.
- **"Footballization of politics" [01:01].** Politics-as-tribal-team-support. Evidence for the migration of sports-fan psychology into political identity. Doing genuine analytical work, not just color.
- **Sutherland's "measured opinion" Twitter experiment [02:02].** He posted deliberately moderate takes ("I quite like Piers Morgan in small doses"; would apply equally to Jordan Peterson, Jeremy Corbyn). Result: no traction, and it "feels really weird." **Evidence for:** moderation is socially unrewarded and psychologically uncomfortable — the demand-side reason spirals go unopposed. Self-report, n=1, but honest and illustrative.
- **Rob Manuel's "@f*sh*le / non-opinion" [03:02].** The anonymous middle-aged left-wing academic who admits he "really love[s] Piers" but couldn't say so in his milieu without being "practically cancelled." **Evidence for:** preference falsification — the true distribution of opinion is hidden. Strong support: anonymity reveals what open discourse suppresses.
- **Road rage / parking misunderstanding [05:03].** He fumed at a driver who beeped, assuming hostility, only to realize the beep was a cautious warning. **Evidence for:** perspective-taking and attribution error. Supports the "understand others' viewpoint" point; only tangentially about signalling.
- **Curb Your Enthusiasm / Chekhov's gun [06:04].** Larry David's radio-hooting misunderstanding echoes Sutherland's road rage; the "Chekhovian gun" structure. Illustrative/entertaining, low analytical load.
- **Seinfeld's failure in Britain vs America [06:04–07:04].** Brits "never got into Seinfeld"; a "very Jewish New York thing"; average Americans know some Yiddish, Brits in Wales/Bristol don't know "chutzpah." Evidence for cultural-context dependence of humor and reference. Largely host-driven cultural aside.
- **Hugh Edwards vs Dan Wooten (host-led, [08:04]).** Two 20-year-olds with journalistic aspirations; one takes the BBC path (rewarded for seriousness with a knighthood), one the tabloid path (rewarded for scoops with money); 20 years later they are "two totally different people" — and, ironically, both fell in sex scandals. **Evidence for:** incentives, not innate character, produce the person; we unfairly judge the product of a reward structure. Strong illustration of environment-shapes-identity; the shared-scandal coda slightly undercuts the neatness.
- **Piers Morgan as serious both-sides broadcaster [08:04].** Host's claim that Morgan, though "tabloid and shock jock," was the rare voice giving both Israel/Palestine sides serious airtime to a huge audience — so the reflexive "what a bastard" judgment is unfair. Evidence for judging people by function/output rather than surface style.

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **On extremism.** Orthodox view: online extremism reflects sincerely held, increasingly radical *beliefs*. Sutherland's inversion: it's mostly *signalling behaviour* — people escalate to prove membership, not because their convictions have genuinely intensified. **Steelman objection:** signalling and sincere belief are not cleanly separable; performing extremism repeatedly can *cause* real radicalization (self-perception / cognitive dissonance), so treating it as "mere" signalling understates the danger and the reality of the beliefs produced.
- **On moderation.** Orthodox view: reasoned, balanced takes are the mark of the thoughtful person and should win the argument. Sutherland's inversion: balance is a *losing* strategy — it earns no traction, feels wrong to write, and exposes you to attack from both sides with no defenders. **Steelman objection:** this risks a counsel of despair; it treats the social-media reward structure as fixed rather than as a designable environment, and platforms/norms *can* be re-architected to reward good-faith disagreement.
- **On character and judgment (host-amplified).** Orthodox view: public figures like Dan Wooten are bad people who made bad choices. Sutherland's inversion: they are largely the output of the incentive gradient they were placed on; the same raw material on the BBC gradient becomes a knighted national treasure. **Steelman objection:** this over-determines behaviour by environment and risks excusing genuine agency and wrongdoing — the shared sex-scandal endings suggest disposition still matters.
- **On platforming.** Sutherland's stated preference [04:02]: "I would like to hear far more trans voices... rather than people speaking on their behalf" — a contrarian jab at self-appointed advocates ("white people... seeking to take up the cudgels on behalf of some group as though that group can't speak for themselves"). **Steelman objection:** he concedes there *are* groups that can't speak for themselves and are under-represented, so the principle needs a criterion he doesn't supply.

## 7. Practical applications

- **(a) Marketing / branding.** Understand that consumer allegiance to brands operates on the football/tribal logic: costly, visible signals of belonging matter more than moderate approval. But note the inverse warning for public communication — a brand or spokesperson taking measured positions in a polarized space gets "no traction" and gets hit "from both sides"; neutrality is not a safe harbour, it's an exposed one.
- **(b) Product & pricing / platform design.** The purity-spiral and preference-falsification dynamics are *products of the reward architecture* (engagement rewards extremity; identity punishes moderation). This licenses a choice-architecture intervention: anonymity surfaces suppressed honest opinion (the "non-opinion" mechanism), so designing channels that lower the social cost of expressing balanced views could de-escalate spirals. Rewards, not exhortation, change behaviour.
- **(c) Everyday persuasion.** Practise deliberate perspective-taking before reacting — assume the "beep" is cautionary, not hostile [05:03]. When judging people, ask what incentive gradient produced them rather than attributing a fixed character (the journalist lesson). And recognize that your own strongly-signalled position may be tribal display rather than reasoned conviction.

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **Signalling as the master key** — recurs throughout Sutherland's work (*Alchemy*'s "costly signalling," peacocks, expensive advertising as a credibility guarantee); here applied to political/moral extremism rather than commerce.
- **Psycho-logic vs logic** — the road-rage misattribution and "measured opinion feels wrong" both show behaviour governed by social/psychological logic, not rational calculation; a core *Alchemy* theme.
- **Anthropological lens on the "bizarre"** — football, face-painting, tribal display framed as anthropology; Sutherland habitually treats human behaviour as an alien would.
- **The unrewarded good** — moderation, like many high-value behaviours in *Alchemy*, is under-supplied because it isn't legible or rewarded; connects to his broader critique of over-optimizing for measurable metrics.
- **Incentives sculpt identity over time** — the journalist thought-experiment prefigures his frequent point that context and incentives, not innate traits, explain behaviour.
- **Both-sides / steelmanning as civic virtue** — the Piers Morgan defense and "hear trans voices directly" echo a recurring anti-tribal, pro-viewpoint-diversity stance.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is **strongest** where signalling theory meets observable behaviour: the claim that moderation earns no traction and feels transgressive is both falsifiable and repeatedly confirmed by anyone's experience of social media, and the football/purity-spiral framing genuinely explains why allied factions attack each other (the Andrew Doyle case). The costly-signalling logic — a signal must exceed the baseline to be credible, forcing escalation — is a real, well-grounded mechanism. It is **weakest** where it drifts into unfalsifiable just-so territory: "biological imperative" is asserted, not evidenced, and the framework can explain *any* behaviour post hoc (extremism = signalling; moderation = failed signalling), which is a hallmark of an over-flexible theory. The reliance on n=1 anecdotes (his own tweets, his road rage) and analogies rather than data means the causal story is plausible but unproven. What would change the picture: evidence on whether performed extremism *converts into* sincere belief over time (which would reframe "mere signalling" as genuine radicalization), and any natural experiment showing that changing platform reward structures measurably shifts the extremity distribution — which would move the thesis from clever description to actionable, testable claim.
