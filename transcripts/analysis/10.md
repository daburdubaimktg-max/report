# 10. Behavioural Economics, Humans and Advertising

## 1. Core thesis
Sutherland's central argument is that human behaviour does not obey physical laws — it is disproportionate, oblique and often perverse — so the most direct, expensive, "stuff"-based solution to a problem is frequently worse than a tiny, cheap psychological intervention. Because value itself is subjective and coloured by two million years of evolution, the true job of advertising and persuasion is not to add value by manipulating reality but to *retrieve* and heighten the appreciation of what already exists, by creating complementary goods and reframing perception. Institutions systematically miss this because they are biased toward "grandstanding" rational solutions they can justify to a committee.

## 2. Argument structure

**Premise 1 — People are biased toward solving problems with "stuff."** "We seem to have a massive bias both as individuals and as institutions towards solving problems with stuff with engineering with technology with gadgetry or possibly with legislation with compulsion and they're always the last resort we seem to adopt is actually psychological solutions" [01:00].

**Inference — This bias is driven by institutional need for rational justification.** "It is particularly institutions far more certain individuals who thrive on a rational justification of actions to each other so every single committee or organization within a body has to explain itself rationally rather than emotionally" [04:01]. He labels this "physics Envy" [04:01].

**Evidence (by analogy/anecdote) — Human behaviour is not physical.** The perverse-incentive squirrel-farming anecdote [05:02] and the paying-children-to-paint study [06:03] establish that direct incentives can invert. He reframes the correct model: not physics but climatology — "it's small butterfly's wings effects have enormous effects on human behavior" [06:03].

**Application — Three developments make this urgent for advertising:** (1) digital media enabling interventions; (2) a shift from attitudinal to behaviourist problems ("most people already have the intent to do the right thing what they patiently fail to do is to back that up with action") [08:06]; (3) the rise of behavioural economics, "Daniel Kahneman being the founding father" [11:06]. The train-vs-car asymmetric-decision example [09:06–10:06] is his worked illustration.

**Inference — Value is subjective, and capitalism perversely destroys appreciation.** "value is subjective... our perception of value as human beings is colored by two million years of evolution" [18:10]. As efficient production lowers price, perceived value falls (coach travel, frozen food, TVs) [19:10–20:10].

**Conclusion — The job of advertising is to retrieve value, not add it,** via complementary goods (Becker & Murphy) [21:10], contextual targeting, disproportionate detail, and costly signalling (Peacock's tail) [40:19].

The reasoning leans heavily on analogy and anecdote (squirrels, weddings, Pernod, peacocks) with a thin spine of cited research (Kahneman, Becker/Murphy, Thaler/Sunstein, Duflo, Ariely). The leaps mostly hold as *existence proofs* — that psychological interventions can beat expensive ones — but he generalises from vivid single cases to universal claims without quantifying how often the small solution actually wins.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Perverse incentives / Goodhart-type effects** — the squirrel-tail bounty and pageview bonus [05:02]. He uses it to show incentives designed on physical logic invert in human systems.
- **Motivation crowding-out / overjustification effect** (Deci, Lepper & Greene) — "if you actually reward children for painting pictures they actually stopped doing it earlier than people who are doing it for fun" [06:03]; rewarding creative tasks "rebrands that activity as work" [05:02].
- **Behavioural economics / bounded rationality** — attributed explicitly to **Kahneman** ("the founding father... he won a Nobel Prize for economics when he's not actually an economist he's a psychologist") [11:06]. Core claim: "the context the medium and the interface within which a decision is taken may have a far greater effect on the decision we make than the long-term consequences" [11:06].
- **Present bias / immediacy bias (hyperbolic discounting)** — "immediacy bias... people are disproportionately biased in their lizard brain but what does this decision feel like right now up front" [13:08]; the wedding example dramatizes upfront cost dominating long-term benefit.
- **Choice architecture / framing** — the fish-chicken-beef waiter example [14:08–15:08] is a compromise/decoy effect (extremeness aversion); the credit-card application-form redesign beating an APR cut [12:07].
- **Decoy effect / extremeness aversion** (Ariely, Simonson) — beef makes chicken seem the acceptable healthy middle; "just bring out a look large burger and actually everything else on the menu practically seems like rocket" [15:08].
- **Scarcity heuristic** (Cialdini) — "because something is rare and expensive it must be valuable" (rhino horn, tiger's claw) [18:10].
- **Anchoring / price-quality inference** — cheaper goods are devalued; consumer surplus "generates no human pleasure or appreciation whatsoever" [21:10].
- **Nudge / libertarian paternalism** (Thaler & Sunstein) — cited by name, "paternalistic libertarianism" [32:14]; Save More Tomorrow pension [33:15–34:15].
- **Advertising as complementary goods** (Gary Becker & Kevin Murphy) — cited by name [21:10]; advertising creates the "lettuce the bun the fries" around the core good.
- **Costly signalling / the handicap principle** (Zahavi) — the Peacock's tail, "what Darwinian is call self-handicapping" [40:19]; advertising as a reputation-to-lose signal.
- **Reputation / repeated games** — tourist restaurant vs. reputation-holding business [41:20–42:21]; "you're much more comfortable... buying a second-hand car from the Archbishop of York than from a bird you just met in the pub" [40:19]. (Implicitly Akerlof's market-for-lemons and signalling à la Spence.)
- **Obliquity** (John Kay) — cited by name; "the businesses which seek to make the most profit are not the most profitable" [16:08].
- **Uncertainty/duration and the psychology of waiting** — dot-matrix boards; "a 12-minute wait when you know it's 12 minutes is actually less stressful than a six minute wage when you're uncertain" [30:14–31:14].
- **Costly-sacrifice signalling in gift-giving** — flowers/jewellery valued *because* men don't want them; "it's actual evidence that he loves you that he's prepared to buy you something that he doesn't like" [42:21].

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **18th-century French squirrel bounty** [05:02] — bounty on tails caused squirrel *farming*; evidence for perverse incentives. Genuinely supports the "human systems invert" claim, though the tale is likely apocryphal/embellished (rhetorical work as much as data).
- **Paying children to paint** [06:03] — evidence for crowding-out; a real research finding, legitimately supportive.
- **Train vs. car asymmetric decision** [09:06–11:06] — the choice to drive is made in the car, biasing toward driving; moving the booking decision a day earlier de-biases it. Strong illustration of choice-context; genuinely supports contextual intervention (though empirically asserted, not demonstrated).
- **Credit-card form redesign vs. dropping APR** [12:07] — interface beats economics; supports the behavioural-economics thesis rhetorically.
- **Wedding / marriage aversion** [13:08–14:08] — reframes male marriage-reluctance as aversion to the *wedding* (upfront cost). Comic and admittedly "half" believed; does rhetorical work for immediacy bias.
- **Fish/chicken/beef waiter** [14:08–15:08] — decoy/compromise effect; a clean illustration but presented as anecdote.
- **Rhino horn / tiger's claw / scarcity** [18:10] — evidence that human value-perception is "wacko" and worth meddling with.
- **Coach travel, frozen food, cheap TVs, Iceland, National Express** [19:10–21:10] — efficiency lowers price and thus perceived value; supports the "retrieve value" thesis. Partly genuine economic observation, partly assertion.
- **Sunny D / "Tang"-style price discrimination reference** [19:10] (garbled transcription) — invoked for subjectivity of value.
- **Shell County Guides (John Betjeman)** [23:12–24:12] — advertising as complementary good enhancing motoring; a real historical case, strongly on-point.
- **MySkyStatus, non-metallic braces for BA** [25:13–26:13] — complementarity improving travel experience; his own agency examples, illustrative.
- **Stockholm hotel lift with music-genre buttons** [26:13–27:14] — ~£1,000 branding beating $25m refurbishments; disproportionality, strong example.
- **Virgin Atlantic "pinched from" salt & pepper pots** [27:14] — memorability for trivial cost; strong.
- **Amex "member since" date** [27:14–28:14] — two digits driving huge loyalty value; strong disproportionality case.
- **Eurostar £6bn for 40 mins faster** [27:14–29:14] — engineers optimise numerical journey time; his supermodels-with-champagne / New Orleans paddle-boat riffs argue for optimising *experience*. Rhetorically brilliant, deliberately absurd, makes the point about mis-measured value.
- **Esther Duflo — lentils + social inoculation events** [29:14–30:14] — tiny interventions eradicate poverty; real development-economics work (RCTs), his strongest cited empirical support.
- **Speed cameras / smiley-frowny signs** [30:14] — cheaper feedback beats punishment; supports nudge-over-coercion.
- **London Underground dot-matrix boards** [30:14–31:14] — managing wait perception beats adding trains; strong, memorable.
- **Terminal 5 signage / Heathrow Express brick wall** [32:14–33:15] — magnificent building, atrocious detail; argues for a "director of detail."
- **Save More Tomorrow pension (Thaler/Benartzi)** [33:15–35:16] — committing future pay rises raised saving by "over 200%"; real, cited, his best format-change evidence.
- **Virgin trains contextual traffic-jam posters** [35:16] — "target moment not a target who"; supports contextual digital.
- **Gloucestershire florist — flowers in unexpected places** [35:16–36:17] — context creates surprise/value; illustrative.
- **Shreddies "Diamond Shreddies"** [38:17–40:19] — same product, new shape/frame caused a "major sales uplift"; his purest demonstration of creating value through perception alone; the "New Coke moment" compromise is a nice coda.
- **Jamie White's leather-vs-cloth-seats thought experiment** [40:19] — £500 on leather seats vs. £500 advertising cloth seats as cool; if value is subjective the two are "absolutely equivalent" (he prefers the advertising route "if you're a cow").
- **Archbishop of York vs. pub stranger; tourist restaurant** [40:19–42:21] — reputation/repeated-game signalling; strong intuitive support for costly-signalling theory of advertising.

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Orthodoxy:** Bigger problems need bigger, well-funded solutions. **Inversion:** "small things have huge effect" — a £1,000 lift or dot-matrix board beats multi-million refurbishments and extra trains [26:13–31:14]. **Steelman objection:** survivorship bias — he cites the cheap interventions that worked; the graveyard of cheap gimmicks that did nothing is invisible, and some problems genuinely do need capital (you cannot nudge your way to more track capacity forever).

- **Orthodoxy:** Rational persuasion and better arguments sell products. **Inversion:** "it's not altogether clear that persuasion is actually very persuasive... people don't do what they say they believe they do what's convenient" [24:12–25:13]; better to create complementarity or ease of purchase. **Steelman:** some categories (B2B, considered high-cost purchases) genuinely are argued into, and dismissing persuasion risks over-correcting.

- **Orthodoxy:** Maximising profit / shareholder value is how you run a business. **Inversion (via Kay's obliquity):** "the businesses which seek to make the most profit are not the most profitable"; shareholder-value focus is "completely inimitable to the business of creating business value" [16:08–17:08]. **Steelman:** oblique success stories are selected after the fact; plenty of "big ideal" companies also failed, and the claim is hard to falsify.

- **Orthodoxy:** Lower prices and greater efficiency straightforwardly increase welfare (consumer surplus). **Inversion:** consumer surplus "generates no human pleasure whatsoever" and falling prices destroy appreciation, so efficiency "actually failed to translate into human happiness" [21:10–22:12]. **Steelman:** this treats appreciation as the only welfare metric and ignores that cheap goods let poor people *have the thing at all* — a large, real welfare gain he brushes past.

- **Orthodoxy:** Coercion/penalties (fines, penalty points) change behaviour. **Inversion:** smiley-face feedback beats penalties; nudges beat compulsion [30:14]. **Steelman:** nudges have limits and can be gamed; some behaviours (drink-driving) may genuinely need enforcement.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Reframe the brief from "how do I persuade people to buy?" to "what is the ketchup/bun/fries — the complementary good — for my product?" [24:12].
- Invest in trivial, memorable details ("pinched from Virgin Atlantic," lift music buttons, "member since") — disproportionate distinction for near-zero cost [26:13–28:14].
- Reframe perception of an existing product rather than changing it (Diamond Shreddies) — "make new things familiar and familiar things new" [37:17–40:19].
- Advertise contextually — target the *moment*, not just the person (traffic-jam train posters; flowers in unexpected places) [35:16–36:17].
- Spend on beauty/reputation as costly signalling — a visible reputation to lose is what gives buyers confidence [40:19–42:21].

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Move the decision point to where it favours the rational choice (book train + parking a day ahead) [10:06–11:06].
- Design the interface/form/default, not just the economics — Save More Tomorrow, credit-card forms; change *when and how* people commit [12:07][33:15].
- Beware perverse incentives and reward structures that crowd out intrinsic motivation [05:02–06:03].
- Recognise the value-destroying effect of low price and use advertising to "retrieve value" for efficient, cheap-to-produce goods (coach travel, frozen food) [19:10–35:16].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Reduce uncertainty rather than the objective cost — tell people the wait, don't just shorten it (dot-matrix logic) [30:14].
- Appeal to better judgment with feedback (smiley faces) instead of threats [30:14].
- Use compromise/decoy framing — a premium option makes the middle option look reasonable [14:08–15:08].
- Appoint a "director of detail" — give someone tiny budget but real power to fix the small irritating things everyone is too grand to address [31:14–33:15].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **"Solve with psychology, not stuff/engineering/legislation"** — the master frame of *Alchemy*; recurs as the anti-"physics envy" thesis.
- **Butterfly-wings / disproportionality** — small inputs, huge effects; likely recurs as the core of his "psycho-logic."
- **Value is subjective / retrieve-don't-add value** — foundational; ties to later material on perceived vs. objective value.
- **Costly signalling & the Peacock's tail** — recurs whenever he defends "wasteful" advertising and reputation.
- **Complementary goods (Becker/Murphy)** — his non-persuasion theory of advertising; likely revisited.
- **Choice architecture / context over consequences (Kahneman, Thaler)** — the behavioural-economics backbone across the series.
- **Obliquity (Kay) / anti-shareholder-value** — recurs in his critique of narrow corporate rationality.
- **Anti-measurement / "no one told peacocks about accountancy"** — his standing objection to quantifying everything.
- **Immediacy/present bias & the "lizard brain"** — recurs in decision-timing arguments.

## 9. Critical evaluation

The argument is strongest as an *existence proof*: Sutherland decisively shows that cheap psychological interventions *can* outperform expensive material ones (dot-matrix boards, Save More Tomorrow, Diamond Shreddies, Duflo's lentils), and these examples are backed by genuine research or documented results. His anti-"physics envy" critique of institutional rationality is sharp and well-targeted. The argument is weakest where it becomes unfalsifiable and selection-biased: he cites the tiny interventions that worked without a denominator of those that didn't, so "small things have huge effects" is asserted more than measured. The claim that consumer surplus "generates no human pleasure whatsoever" overreaches — it ignores that cheap goods expand access, which is itself a massive welfare gain, and treats subjective appreciation as the sole metric of value. Much of the load is carried by charismatic anecdote (squirrels, Pernod, weddings, supermodels on Eurostar) rather than data, and the obliquity/anti-shareholder-value claim resists disconfirmation. What would change the picture: controlled comparisons showing how *often* cheap psychological interventions beat capital solutions (and at what magnitude), plus honest accounting of nudge failures and cases where his own agency's "complementarity" interventions produced no measurable lift.
