# 30. Why Has Advertising Become Political?

## 1. Core thesis
Advertising has become politicised because the people who make it are a demographic outlier — young, disproportionately high in the Big Five trait of "openness" — who understand the human urge to *stand out* but not the equally powerful urge to *fit in*, and so they project their own values onto mainstream brands whose audiences don't share them. Sutherland's deeper argument is that most of these campaigns (Bud Light, Gillette, Jaguar) are neither the coordinated conspiracy the right imagines nor the noble mission the left imagines; they are the predictable output of an over-narrow social bubble, and the real lesson is that *good intentions guarantee nothing about outcomes* in complex systems. The through-line uniting advertising, economics, journalism and the NHS is that we optimise the wrong metrics and ignore how information flows (and gets distorted) inside systems.

## 2. Argument structure
Sutherland reasons less by data than by a chain of analogies and system-dynamics claims, each licensing the next:

- **Premise:** It is reasonable for advertising to reflect the population, but "makes perfect sense on an individual level... fails collectively" — a coordination failure. [00:01]
- **Evidence (anecdotal):** The ethnic mix of UK commercials reflects London (roughly 1:1 black:Indian) rather than the country as a whole (nearer a 2-3:1 ratio) [01:01]; Bud Light's Dylan Mulvaney "scandal" was one influencer can among a mass mailing, "synthesized by people on the right" [04:03]; Gillette insulted "your own target audience." [04:03]
- **Inference:** These aren't conspiracies but the output of a workforce that is "disproportionately whacky high on openness," young, and declining in openness only with age. [13:09]
- **Bridging concept:** The two human motivations — "the urge to stand out and the urge to fit in" (crediting planner Kevin Chesters) — and marketers only understand the former. [15:10]
- **Generalisation:** The explore/exploit trade-off (bees, evolution, foraging, business) — actually a complementary "yin and yang," not a trade-off — explains why radical moves (Jaguar) can be legitimate when a category is disrupted. [29:24–34:28]
- **Conclusion:** "The purpose of the system is what it does" (Stafford Beer) — well-intentioned interventions in complex systems routinely produce counterproductive outcomes (reactance, DEI backlash, negativity bias in journalism, the NHS incentive structure).

The reasoning is mostly analogical/anecdotal. The leap that *does* hold: the openness/self-projection explanation is internally consistent and modestly falsifiable. The leaps that strain: he generalises from single anecdotes (his sister-in-law, one car-park incident in LA) to national character, and the "bees ignore the waggle dance" story does heavy rhetorical lifting for a very broad conclusion about corporate strategy.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Big Five / OCEAN, esp. Openness to Experience** (trait psychology; Costa & McCrae lineage). His central mechanism: marketers self-select for high openness, which declines with age, so they are permanently out of step with mainstream (older, lower-openness) consumers. He gives it an evolutionary gloss — exploration pays in youth (more remaining life to benefit) and less in age. [13:09–14:10]

- **Explore/exploit trade-off** (reinforcement learning / behavioural ecology / bandit problems). He twists it, insisting it is *not* a trade-off but a complementary "yin and yang": you can't exploit well without refreshing knowledge by exploring, and can't afford to explore unless you exploit discoveries. Applied to marketing/innovation as the corporate "epicenter for exploration." [29:24–34:28]

- **Stand out vs. fit in** (his own framing, crediting planner **Kevin Chesters**). Maps onto costly signalling / conformity. He argues advertising has served prestige/"rivalrous display" brands better than mainstream brands because practitioners neglect the fitting-in motive. [15:10]

- **Psychological reactance** (Jack Brehm). His sharpest tool here: behaviours people would willingly adopt trigger a "counter" reaction if instructed "in the wrong tone of voice" — so well-intentioned moralising is "well intentioned but counterproductive." [39:34]

- **Negativity bias** (Baumeister; Rozin & Royzman). Applied to journalism: newsstand sales reward "country's gone to the dogs" content, but this is miscalibrated for subscription publications, where readers, like friends, don't want a "Doom Monger." [55:44]

- **Signalling / conspicuous consumption / Veblen goods** (Veblen; Spence; Zahavi's costly signalling). Implicit in "rivalrous displays," in cars as "a very visible part of yourself" (Tesla/Musk sales decline in Europe), and the Jaguar super-premium play. [37:31–38:33]

- **Goodhart's law / metric fixation** (Goodhart; Marilyn Strathern). The finance function optimises "deterministic gains to efficiency," buying short-term predictability "at the price of long-term fragility or irrelevance." The waiting list is "not the correct... proxy metric" for NHS effectiveness. [31:25, 50:40]

- **Cybernetics & "the purpose of the system is what it does" (POSIWID)** (Stafford Beer; Norbert Wiener; via Dan Davies' *The Unaccountability Machine*). Systems viewed through information flow rather than money flow; interventions in complex systems aren't "simple Newtonian relationship[s] between input and output." [44:35–45:35]

- **Information asymmetry** (Akerlof; Spence; Stiglitz). His NHS and journalism examples: it is career-suicidal for a doctor to say the system is fine (NHS) or bad (US hospital), so reported information is systematically biased. [46:37]

- **Rent-seeking vs. value creation** (Tullock; Henry George — he calls himself "a georgist"). Economics recognises the difference but never taxes the two differently; hence his wealth-vs-income tax argument. [67:54–68:54]

- **Satisficing** (Herbert Simon). Explicitly names "satisficer brands" — Starbucks, Costa, Premier Inn, easyJet — that "don't raise the ceiling but... raise the floor." [15:10 concept; 71:59 term]

- **Homo economicus critique / bounded rationality** (von Mises, Kahneman, John Kay). Purely economically rational actors "do exist... you don't want them as your customers." [64:52]

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **Bud Light / Dylan Mulvaney (2023).** One personalised can in a mass influencer mailing, amplified by the press "for its own convenience" as though Mulvaney had become "the new face of Bud Light." Evidence FOR: manufactured/synthesised scandal + the marketing exec's leaked "we don't like those people... their sense of humor is out of date" comment supplying the real damage. Genuinely supports his "not a conspiracy, but a bubble" claim, though the exec's own words show the ideological self-projection he diagnoses. [04:03–07:05]

- **Gillette "toxic masculinity" ad.** "Almost to the point of insulting your own target audience... extraordinary weird." Evidence FOR: good intentions ≠ good outcomes; brands alienating 50% of their audience. Rhetorically it's his cleanest example of self-projection. [04:03, 43:35]

- **Dove "Campaign for Real Beauty" (Ogilvy — his own).** The *counter*-example: a cosmetics brand credibly occupying "beauty without artifice," "entirely consistent with what that brand did." Evidence FOR: brand purpose works when it authentically fits the brand. Doing honest work — a case where the claim genuinely holds. [03:03]

- **Coke "Hilltop / I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing" (Vietnam era).** Diverse "ideological" advertising long predates wokeism; Coke was historically Democrat, Pepsi Republican (Nixon-era Pepsi in the White House); Trump broke the pattern (Diet Coke button on the desk). Evidence FOR: politicised advertising is not new, deflating moral panic. Legitimate historical context. [02:02–03:03]

- **Jaguar "Copy Nothing" rebrand.** His most developed defence. Not an ad but a zero-media-budget "brand film"; the slogan is founder Sir William Lyons' (from ~1928, not modern). He defends it because (a) Jaguar is a Challenger/disrupted brand (electrification = the iPod moment for cars), (b) it's a low-volume super-premium maker selling ~7,000 US cars/year, (c) "you can have loads of people loving your brand but if they ain't buying it" — the red-telephone-box problem. Evidence FOR: explore/exploit + brand-consistency rules should be broken under category disruption. Does real analytical work, though it doubles as a defence of his own industry. [16:10–29:24]

- **Electrification as disruption.** ICE drivetrain ~230 moving parts vs. ~6-7 for electric; his own service found "there isn't any other stuff"; Audi-petrol → Škoda-electric migration; classic-car electrification businesses (electric 1970s Range Rovers). Evidence FOR: category disruption dissolves established brand hierarchies. Concrete and persuasive. [19:15–24:21]

- **Michael Jordan — "Republicans buy sneakers too."** Refusing to endorse a Democrat. Evidence FOR: don't alienate 50% of the market. Then hedged: "doesn't seem to have hurt Taylor Swift... probably hurt Elon" (Tesla Europe sales). Honest about the limits of his own rule. [37:31–38:33]

- **The LA car-park stand-off.** British "20-second bargaining" (hands up, "my bad") vs. American rule-based incomprehension when something "unusual" breaks the rules. Evidence FOR: Americans as absolutist/rule-bound. Pure anecdote doing heavy characterisation work; entertaining but weak as evidence. [10:08–12:09]

- **Ben & Jerry's lecturing on Palestine while owned by Unilever.** Evidence FOR: the incongruity/hypocrisy of brand moralising. Rhetorical, and mildly self-implicating (Unilever is a client-type). [36:29–37:31]

- **The NHS vs. US hospital / Mayo Clinic.** Opposite information incentives: US doctors must exaggerate quality (patient is customer), NHS staff must exaggerate crisis (government is funder). Evidence FOR: information asymmetry as a systemic distortion. His most original applied point. [46:37–47:39]

- **Farmers' markets.** "You buy direct from the manufacturer but you pay more"; "you take a Tesco Metro and you make it deliberately inefficient." Evidence FOR: markets are "street theatre" — performative, relational, about discernment and being seen, not efficiency. Illustrates "the opposite of a good idea is another good idea." [41:34–43:35]

- **Drone delivery (Bobby Healey, Ireland; "low altitude economy").** ~10p last-mile cost, 60mph, lands on a trampoline. Sutherland admits he was "totally wrong" to dismiss it. The predictable killer: one dog hurt by a "falling tin of baked beans" becomes a five-day news story, while "every drone is five miles of moped taken off the road." Evidence FOR: negativity bias + harm-aversion throttling beneficial innovation. Strong, self-aware example. [75:03–76:03]

- **Satisficer brands (Starbucks, Costa, Premier Inn, easyJet, Holiday Inn Express).** They "killed off the shit part of the market" and "raise the floor." The B&Q Tunbridge Wells coffee "better than the Ritz in 1984." Evidence FOR: value brands as an under-appreciated public good. Persuasive, though he concedes it steamrollers "mom and pop" businesses ("the good ones survive"). [71:59–73:00]

- **US Democrats as "the biggest marketing cock-up you can possibly imagine."** Biden's re-run "should have been suppressed"; poor candidate selection; failure to understand Main Street. Evidence FOR: his stand-out/fit-in and bubble thesis applied to politics. [58:48–59:49]

- **Conservative Party rebrand / Georgism.** Go heavy on localism/devolution (Kent mayors), break the "lawfare and economics stranglehold," and — the electorally impossible move — "tax wealth more and income less." Property as "a massive great sump"; the landlord of his daughter's house "never has to work again" from a 1983 purchase. Evidence FOR: rent-seeking vs. value-creation distinction. Substantive but openly self-described as partly "self-serving alone of bollocks." [1:00:51–1:14:02]

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Brand consistency.** Orthodox (Byron Sharp, Mark Ritson): protect long-built brand assets; consistency compounds. His inversion: consistency rules should be *broken* for Challenger brands and categories under "cataclysmic disruption" (Jaguar). Steelman objection: Sharp's evidence base (mental/physical availability, distinctiveness) is empirical across thousands of brands; Jaguar's zero-media brand film may generate awareness without the buyers to convert it — famous ≠ bought, which is exactly his own red-telephone-box worry turned against him.

- **Efficiency as the goal of business.** Orthodox: financial discipline, deterministic efficiency gains, shareholder value. His inversion: efficiency without exploration buys "short-term predictable gains... at the price of long-term fragility or irrelevance"; the 150-year survivors are marketing-led (Unilever, Nestlé, P&G). Steelman: survivorship bias — plenty of exploration-heavy firms also died; efficiency and exploration aren't as cleanly opposed as the bee parable implies.

- **Negativity sells / journalism must be adversarial.** Orthodox: bad news drives engagement; the good journalist is Woodward-and-Bernstein-suspicious ("who is this liar and why is he lying to me?"). His inversion: negativity is calibrated for newsstand impulse buys, not subscriptions; over-negativity is a bug carried over from a dead business model. Steelman: subscribers to *The Spectator*/*The Week* self-select for balance — this may describe niche titles, not mass media where outrage still monetises.

- **Homo economicus.** Orthodox: rational self-interest models behaviour. His inversion: perfectly rational actors "exist... you don't want them as your customers" (they defect for 3% cheaper). Steelman: he's attacking a straw man — few serious economists claim consumers are literally that; behavioural economics already absorbed this.

- **Wealth vs. income taxation.** Orthodox: tax income; property is sacrosanct. His Georgist inversion: tax unearned asset-price gains and non-renewable consumption, tax labour less. Steelman: he concedes it's electorally suicidal ("how do you get reelected after you've tanked the housing market") and that landlords don't "all have it easy" — the practical implementation problem is unanswered.

- **Harm aversion.** Orthodox: minimise all foreseeable harm. His inversion: a world optimised so "you're not allowed to serve food to which anybody might hypothetically be allergic" is unliveable; you "can't calibrate the world" to minority rule. Steelman: he grants the exception (nuts on planes, anaphylaxis, trigger warnings) that shows the line is genuinely hard to draw — his own principle admits the hard cases.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Audit whether a campaign speaks to the *fit-in* audience or only flatters the creators' *stand-out* values; assume your marketing team is an openness outlier, not a representative sample. [15:10]
- Brand purpose works only where it's authentically continuous with what the brand does (Dove), not bolted on (Gillette). [03:03–04:03]
- Break consistency rules only under Challenger status or genuine category disruption — otherwise stay true to brand assets. [18:15]
- Beware reactance: the same message delivered as moralising instruction provokes the opposite of compliance. Manage tone. [39:34]
- "Being ignored is a problem" — but "no such thing as bad publicity" is false; distinctiveness must still convert to purchase. [24:21–25:22]

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Under technological disruption, established brand hierarchies dissolve — plan for buyer migration across old status lines (petrol Audi → electric Škoda). [19:15]
- The "satisficer" position (raise the floor, reliable value) is a durable, defensible strategy that kills the bottom of the market. [71:59]
- Protect an exploratory budget deliberately — the "random bees" — even when it looks inefficient; it's insurance against category change, not waste. [33:27]
- Don't let the finance function crowd out exploration; optimise for long-run relevance, not just deterministic efficiency metrics. [31:25]

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Don't align a broad-appeal offering with a divisive political position — "Republicans buy sneakers too"; alienating 50% of your audience is rarely worth it. [37:31]
- Separate intention from outcome — assess what a message *does*, not what it means to do (POSIWID). [45:35]
- Recognise information asymmetries in any institution before trusting its self-reporting: who is the "customer," and which direction are they incentivised to distort? [46:37]
- Winning the argument ≠ being right; respect that "the opposite of a good idea is another good idea." [41:34]

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **"Opposite of a good idea is another good idea"** — his signature psycho-logic principle from *Alchemy*; recurs wherever he defends non-rational/relational value.
- **Explore/exploit & the random bees** — the foraging/innovation parable; likely a recurring engine across the series and central to *Alchemy*'s defence of "wasteful" behaviour.
- **Stand out vs. fit in** — costly signalling and conformity; ties to his broader signalling theory (peacocks, Veblen goods, "expensiveness as a feature").
- **Satisficing / "raise the floor" brands** — recurs in his defence of scale brands and value chains.
- **Homo economicus critique / John Kay's cat** — his standing attack on neoclassical rationality; expect across most talks.
- **Georgism, rent-seeking vs. value creation** — his political-economy hobby-horse; likely reappears on tax, housing, productivity.
- **POSIWID / cybernetics / information flow** — Dan Davies' *Unaccountability Machine*; a systems lens likely echoed in NHS, GDP-per-head, and metric-fixation discussions.
- **Harm aversion / drone "low-altitude economy"** — innovation throttled by anecdotal risk and negativity bias; a recurring techno-optimist thread.
- **Negativity bias & the newsstand-vs-subscription distinction** — media economics; a reusable frame.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where Sutherland does structural analysis rather than culture-war commentary: the openness/self-projection account of why marketers misjudge mainstream audiences is genuinely explanatory and modestly testable; the information-asymmetry contrast between US and NHS incentive structures is original and generative; and the Jaguar defence is a disciplined application of explore/exploit that resists easy left/right framing. It is weakest where it leans on single anecdotes carrying national-character conclusions (the LA car park, one sister-in-law) and where the governing metaphors — bees, yin-yang, "the purpose of the system is what it does" — become unfalsifiable: almost any outcome can be retro-fitted as the system's "real" purpose or as neglected exploration, which makes the framework hard to disprove. His self-awareness is a real strength (he flags when he's "being perverse," "self-serving," or was "totally wrong" about drones), but it can also inoculate weak claims against scrutiny. What would change the picture: hard data on whether "Copy Nothing"-style disruption actually converts to sales for Jaguar (versus Sharp/Ritson's distinctiveness evidence); controlled evidence that reactance, not the message content itself, drives brand-purpose backlash; and comparative figures on whether satisficer brands net-raise or net-lower consumer welfare once the displaced independents are counted.
