# 22. Ad Expert Reveals The Worst Mistakes In Marketing: Rory Sutherland

## 1. Core thesis
Businesses systematically misallocate attention and money toward what is easily measurable — bottom-of-funnel conversion, the "sales area," immediate financial return — while starving the things that are genuinely valuable but hard to quantify: post-purchase customer service, brand fame, loyalty, and long-run price power. Sutherland's central argument is that the "quest for perfect measurement to reduce marketing to a kind of Newtonian physics is a bit of a false god," and that the biggest mistakes in marketing are failures of the *removal of negatives* (service, complaints, delivery) rather than deficits in the *addition of positives*.

## 2. Argument structure
Sutherland builds the case cumulatively rather than deductively, moving from concrete e-commerce fixes up to an epistemological point about measurement itself.

- **Premise 1:** "Marketers spend too much time focusing on the addition of positives when a lot of time needs to be spent on the removal of negatives" [00:00]. → **Evidence:** the "world of pain" you enter the moment you deviate from the pre-scripted purchase funnel [01:00–03:00]; hidden phone numbers; single-courier delivery. → **Inference:** the metrics of the post-purchase team ("anything but customer satisfaction… cost reduction") are structurally misaligned with the customer. This is an anecdote/observation-driven leap, but a plausible one.
- **Premise 2 (the load-bearing generalization):** "People generally over-obsess about things which are immediately quantifiable and under-invest in things which are valuable but hard to actually put a figure on" [06:03]. → **Evidence:** loyalty is "much much slower to measure than conversion" [07:03]; 97% of the customer base "aren't in market at any given time" [07:03]; big data "all comes from the same place — the past" [10:07]. → **Inference:** money flows to channels "not because it's more effective there but simply because… it's easier to prove that it has an effect" [07:03].
- **Premise 3 (the corrective, avoiding a straw man):** He explicitly refuses the brand-vs-performance dichotomy, citing Ritson's "both-ism" and even conceding the bottom of the funnel "is the thing you have to optimize first" because advertising into a broken conversion funnel just "introduce[s] more people to a disappointing experience" [08:05–09:06].
- **Conclusion:** Optimize in the order repeat purchase → conversion → acquisition [09:06–10:07]; aim near a 60/40 brand/performance split [11:08]; and accept that perfect measurement is unattainable and its pursuit distorts.

The reasoning leans heavily on analogy (electric cars, Apple) and authority (Binet & Field, Ogilvy, Ritson, Johnson) rather than on data he presents directly. The one place he offers a near-quantitative datum — American Express TV advertising lifting direct-mail response — is recalled loosely ("would not quite double maybe but they increased pretty significantly") [12:08], which is characteristic: he argues *that* the unmeasurable matters, using mostly unmeasured examples.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Loss aversion / negativity bias (implicit).** "Removal of negatives" over "addition of positives" [00:00] and the asymmetry that "when it goes well it's miraculously good but the second anything out of the ordinary happens you enter a world of pain" [03:00]. Draws on Kahneman & Tversky's asymmetric weighting of losses vs gains; Sutherland twists it into an operational rule about where marketing effort should sit.
- **Costly signaling.** Named explicitly re packaging: "possibly there's a little bit of costly signaling involved" — the yellow Selfridges box, shiny backdrop, tissue paper, producing a "halo effect on your perceived value" [04:01]. Rooted in Zahavi (handicap principle) and Spence (market signalling); a wasteful, hard-to-fake gesture certifies quality.
- **Halo effect.** Explicitly invoked — packaging quality bleeds into perceived product value [04:01] (Thorndike/Nisbett).
- **Goodhart's law / surrogation (implicit and structural).** The whole indictment of managing to measurable proxies — "how can we make sure that nobody phones us up… minimize the cost" [01:00–02:00] and money going where effect is *provable* rather than *real* [07:03]. When a measure becomes the target it ceases to be a good measure; Sutherland applies it to the tyranny of quantifiable KPIs.
- **Scarcity.** Discussed as "your shtick" — limited runs, sell-outs, drops [05:02–06:03]. Cialdini's scarcity principle; used both as demand driver and as brand-value signal.
- **Base-rate / market-penetration reality ("moving parade").** 97% out of market at any time; Ogilvy's "you're not advertising to a standing army… [but] a moving parade" [07:03–10:07]. Aligns with Ehrenberg-Bass / Byron Sharp thinking on continuous mental availability.
- **Counterfactual invisibility (implicit).** His epistemological core: price premium and elasticity effects are unmeasurable "because you don't have the counterfactual" [13:09] — you can't observe the sale that wouldn't have happened at that price without the brand.
- **Reality distortion field.** Named re Apple — brand fame buys forgiveness for product flaws and reduces price sensitivity [12:08–13:09]. Sutherland treats fame as an insurance-like asset.
- **Focusing illusion / streetlight effect (implicit).** Over-weighting the immediately quantifiable because it is salient and easy to look at is a focusing illusion applied to management; "easier to prove" ≈ searching under the streetlight.

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **Hidden phone numbers / single-courier e-commerce [01:00–03:00].** What happens: post-purchase responsibility passes to cost-reduction teams; customers denied courier choice. Evidence FOR: misaligned incentives create the "world of pain." The courier point does real analytical work — he identifies a genuine mechanism (blame attribution): "if I'd chosen to have it delivered by Royal Mail and it went missing I'd blame Royal Mail," whereas an imposed courier makes the *retailer* the target of blame. Insightful, not merely rhetorical.
- **Hermes/Evri local drivers [02:00–03:00].** Delivery quality "varies enormously depending on which postcode district you're in." Supports the courier-choice claim and the idea that respecting customer preference offloads risk. Genuine support.
- **Selfridges packaging [04:01].** Yellow interior, logo, tissue paper. Evidence FOR costly signaling and packaging as the moment "a product first becomes a brand." Illustrative rather than proof, but apt.
- **DOAC apparel drop (the host's brand) [05:02–06:03].** Limited runs, sold out every night of a 9-night tour "to the point that we sold the ones on our backs." Sutherland uses it to affirm scarcity and the loss-leader/brand-play logic. This is the interviewee's own example that Sutherland validates rather than one he supplies.
- **Electric vs gasoline cars [09:06–10:07].** The right question isn't monthly sales share but "does anybody who buys an electric car go back to buying a gasoline car" — i.e. repeat/retention reveals the S-curve. Analogy doing heavy lifting for "optimize repeat purchase first"; persuasive but an analogy, not evidence about marketing spend.
- **American Express TV → direct mail [12:08].** Every time Amex advertised in mass media, direct-mail response rates rose "pretty significantly." The talk's best empirical anchor for the brand/performance synergy and the 60/40 case — but quoted from memory and imprecise.
- **Apple "antennagate" [12:08–13:09].** "The famous phone where if you held it in the wrong way it didn't make phone calls" — brand fame let Apple survive a flaw that would be "pretty fatal to lesser brands." Evidence FOR fame as forgiveness and reduced price sensitivity. Strong illustrative example; the unfalsifiable part (how much premium the brand *causes*) is exactly his counterfactual point.

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Orthodox view:** Spend where you can prove ROI; performance/digital marketing is superior because it's trackable. **Sutherland's inversion:** trackability, not effectiveness, is why money flows there — "the money isn't necessarily being spent in the channels it is because it's more effective there but simply because it's easier to prove that it has an effect" [07:03]. **Steelman objection:** accountability exists for a reason; "it's valuable but unmeasurable" is precisely the alibi that excuses wasteful brand spend, and Sutherland offers no way to distinguish genuinely-valuable-unmeasurable from simply-ineffective.
- **Orthodox view:** Marketing's job is customer acquisition (top of funnel) or lead conversion. **His inversion:** "the first thing… you should optimize if you're being an absolute purist is repeat purchase," then conversion, then acquisition [09:06]. **Steelman:** for genuinely new brands there is no repeat base to optimize; the ordering can be circular.
- **Orthodox view:** More data equals better prediction. **His inversion:** "all big data comes from the same place — the past," and after regime shifts like a pandemic its predictive value is limited [10:07]. **Steelman:** most business environments are stable enough that historical data remains highly predictive; treating all forecasting as unreliable overcorrects.
- **Orthodox view (rejected on both sides):** Brand vs performance is either/or. **His position:** false dichotomy; Ritson's "both-ism," ~60/40 brand/performance (Binet & Field) [08:05–11:08]. Here he is contrarian toward *both* purist camps rather than one.
- **Orthodox view:** Give every customer the same standardized, cost-optimized delivery. **His inversion:** let the *paying* customer choose the courier, because choice reassigns blame and respects preference [02:00–03:00].

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Rebalance spend toward brand/mass-media, roughly 60/40, treating brand and performance as mutually reinforcing (Amex effect) rather than rival [11:08–12:08].
- Invest in fame as an insurance asset: it buys customer forgiveness for failures and reduces price sensitivity [12:08–13:09].
- Advertise to the "moving parade" — the ~97% not currently in market — accepting the payoff is unprovable in the short term [07:03].

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Fix the bottom of the funnel *first*: never drive traffic into a poor conversion or fulfilment experience, or you just "introduce more people to a disappointing experience" [08:05].
- Prioritize repeat-purchase economics over acquisition once conversion works [09:06].
- Use packaging as a costly-signal / brand-birth moment — the unboxing halo raises perceived value [04:01].
- Deploy scarcity (limited runs, drops) to build both demand and brand mythology [05:02].
- Offer a flat-fee annual delivery subscription (Amazon Prime equivalent) so loyal customers aren't penalized relative to one-offs [03:00].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Manage the failure path, not just the pitch: "answer the phone" and don't hide your number [00:00–03:00].
- Give people a genuine choice (e.g. of courier) — it transfers responsibility/blame away from you and increases satisfaction [02:00–03:00].
- Beware managing to the measurable proxy; ask what valuable thing you're neglecting because it's hard to count.

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **Removal of negatives > addition of positives** — a recurring Sutherland heuristic; likely echoes across the series and *Alchemy*'s "psycho-logic."
- **Costly signaling / the handicap principle** — a core *Alchemy* pillar (peacocks, flowers, expensive advertising as honesty guarantee).
- **The unmeasurable is undervalued / "false god of measurement"** — his signature epistemological theme; connects to his distrust of spreadsheet-driven, "Newtonian" management.
- **Counterfactuals and the limits of data** — "big data comes from the past"; recurs wherever he attacks over-reliance on quantification and prediction.
- **Scarcity, drops, and brand mythology** — links to broader discussions of meaning, story, and perceived value over utility.
- **Fame as forgiveness / reality distortion (Apple)** — brand-as-asset argument likely to reappear in pricing and reputation discussions.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where Sutherland identifies concrete, mechanism-backed failures: the incentive misalignment of post-purchase teams, and the courier-choice/blame-attribution insight, which is genuinely clever and testable. His epistemological point — that we over-invest in the provable rather than the effective — is important and well supported by the counterfactual-invisibility observation (you cannot measure the premium your brand *caused*). It is weakest precisely where it is most sweeping: "valuable but hard to measure" is close to unfalsifiable, and he supplies no criterion to separate the truly-valuable-unmeasurable from the merely-ineffective, which is exactly the move a self-serving brand agency would want to license. His empirical anchors (Amex, Apple, the 97% figure, 60/40) are cited from authority or memory rather than presented, and the electric-car analogy, while vivid, proves nothing about marketing budgets. What would change the picture: controlled evidence isolating brand-spend effects on price elasticity and lifetime value (the Binet/Field and Ehrenberg-Bass literatures he gestures at), or geo/holdout experiments quantifying the very "unmeasurable" effects he claims can't be measured — the strongest rebuttal to Sutherland is that modern incrementality testing has narrowed the gap between "provable" and "effective" more than his framing allows.
