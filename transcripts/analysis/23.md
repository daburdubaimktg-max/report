# 23. Dirty Little Marketing Secrets That Always Work — Rory Sutherland (4K)

## 1. Core thesis
Most business and public-policy failure stems from privileging what is fast, measurable and quantifiable (cost savings, efficiency, short-term acquisition metrics) over what is slow, tacit and probabilistic (brand, loyalty, opportunity cost, human experience). Sutherland's central argument is that "we're too impatient to be intelligent" — real value lives in the things spreadsheets cannot capture, so the winning move is to defend the un-measurable (the doorman, the branded teddy bear, the lit forecourt) against the seductive logic of subtraction and optimisation.

## 2. Argument structure
Sutherland rarely builds a single linear proof; he argues by accumulation of analogy and anecdote, circling one recurring inference: **the measurable crowds out the important.**

- **Premise:** Humans (and organisations) default to what delivers fast, legible feedback. *Evidence:* email vs. typing pool [08:05–10:08]; acquisition vs. loyalty spend [21:14]; short-term "performance advertising" vs. long-term brand building [20:14]. *Inference:* institutions systematically under-invest in slow-feedback value. *Conclusion:* customer service "got so bad" [20:14] because business discourse only rewards quarterly financials.
- **Premise:** "We think we know what we want but we don't" [15:13]. *Evidence:* Rightmove search behaviour, estate-agent buyers ending up with properties matching "remarkably few" of their criteria [14:12–17:13]. *Inference:* rational-looking digital filtration destroys value because preference is discovered, not pre-specified. This is his strongest *structural* (non-anecdotal) argument, and it holds — it identifies a genuine mechanism (search boundaries hiding "holes" in London, £850k pricing traps).
- **Analogy engine:** He repeatedly leaps from human behaviour to evolutionary biology (dinosaur plumage → wings [08:05]) and back. These analogies illustrate rather than prove; the peacock's-tail-becomes-flight story is evocative but does rhetorical work, not evidential.
- **The costs-vs-opportunity-costs argument** [42:34–45:36] is his cleanest: sins of commission (the stolen Lion bar, £1) trigger inquiry; sins of omission (unlit forecourt, ~£200k lost revenue) go unpunished. The inference — finance functions "pretend opportunities aren't there because they're too nebulous" — is sound and well-supported by the anecdote, though the £200k figure is invented on the spot.

Where the leap is weakest: he generalises from single vivid cases (one forecourt, one AO teddy bear) to systemic claims. He himself flags this self-awareness about evolutionary psychology "jumping the shark" [1:15:09].

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Signalling / costly signalling (Zahavi, Spence):** Early technology "driven by status seeking rather than utility" [07:03]; early car adopters, Apple Vision Pro, the typewriter as professional signalling [08:05–10:08]. He twists Zahavi's handicap principle onto product adoption — the first mover "pays a price" as a costly signal.
- **Sexual selection / handicap principle (Zahavi, Darwin):** Dinosaur plumage evolving for display before flight [08:05] — status signalling "parlayed into" utility.
- **Loss aversion / status quo bias (Kahneman & Tversky):** US two-week vacation as "status quo bias" — nobody argues to *roll back* holiday even though they resist *granting* it [1:23:16]. Also the "once you've experienced the alternative the old behaviour seems ridiculous" asymmetry (CDs→downloads, kettle→boiling tap, EV adoption non-reversion) [05:02–06:03].
- **Anchoring (Tversky & Kahneman):** Property priced at £850k invisible to searchers anchored at 800/900 boundaries [16:13]; "the anchoring and set point that we have" re the teddy bear [44:36].
- **Focusing illusion / what-you-see-is-all-there-is (Kahneman):** "We don't see what we don't see" in Rightmove search [15:13]; finance pretending opportunity costs "aren't there" [45:36].
- **Preference construction / discovery (Slovic, Ariely, Lichtenstein):** "We think we know what we want but we don't"; preferences refined "by a kind of exploratory process of discovery" [15:13].
- **Ergodicity / representative-agent critique (Ole Peters via Gary Stevenson):** Economic models use "single representative agents" so inequality vanishes — "if Bill Gates walks into a football stadium everybody is on average a millionaire" [56:51]; reused for the ULEZ flat-charge injustice [2:02:52].
- **Two-way vs one-way door decisions (Bezos):** Reversible experiments vs irreversible commitments [24:17–25:17]; folds into his probabilistic-vs-deterministic thinking theme.
- **Probabilistic vs deterministic thinking:** "Most business is probabilistic but everybody wants to pretend it's deterministic… every spreadsheet is an act of pretense" [25:17–26:17].
- **Goodhart's law / surrogation (implicit; Charles Goodhart, John Kay):** Companies pursuing "some proxy measure of success other than the marketplace" become detached [1:41:39]. "The purpose of the system is what it does" (Stafford Beer, POSIWID) [47:37].
- **Cobra effect / perverse incentives:** British India cobra bounty; Athens odd/even number-plate rule spawning second cars [1:52:47]. Explicit.
- **Hyperbolic discounting (implicit):** "Urgent crowds out important" — the genie parable [18:13–20:14].
- **Framing effects (Kahneman/Tversky; George Lakoff cited via "Berkeley… George" = George Lakoff):** "Tax relief" framing implies tax is a burden [1:30:25]; "thank you" text for on-time rent [1:28:23].
- **Environmental security hypothesis (human behavioural ecology):** Men under resource stress prefer larger body types; hemline index; canteen hunger study [1:13:07–1:15:09].
- **Narcissism of small differences (Freud):** Colombian/Mexican, Cuban/Puerto Rican tensions [1:22:16].
- **Innovator's dilemma / disruption (Clayton Christensen):** Explicitly named for Jaguar/EV transition — sunk cost in ICE engineering [32:24].
- **Diminishing marginal utility / positional goods (Veblen, Hirsch):** As you get richer, "relative status matters more than absolute value"; top-of-range car costs 25% more but sells for double [1:27:21]. Luxury goods "only work when you have an audience of strangers" [1:12:05].
- **Spend-thrifts vs tight-wads (George Loewenstein):** Pain of paying; skinflints outnumber spendthrifts [2:04:54]. Experiences over goods (Loewenstein/Gilovich).
- **Hypothecation / mental accounting (Dan Ariely, Thaler):** Earmarked taxes paid "more happily" [1:55:50].
- **Nudge / choice architecture (Thaler & Sunstein):** Cookie-banner solution "given to nudge theorists rather than lawyers" [1:53:49]; Singapore/Housing-Association rent-thank-you nudge [1:28:23].

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **Remote-work team meetup → three pay-rise requests** [02:00]: Evidence that in-person "non-critical conversation" surfaces salary comparison; supports his siloing/compartmentalisation point but is a single second-hand anecdote doing heavy rhetorical work.
- **Google Starline 3D video conferencing** [04:01]: Evidence for his complaint that hardware investment in remote work has lagged — genuinely supports the "built environment hasn't caught up with technology" thesis he returns to at [1:46:43].
- **CDs → downloads; kettle → Quooker boiling tap; EV non-reversion** [05:02–06:03]: All illustrate the adoption-asymmetry claim (resistance before, irreversibility after). EV data (adopters "generally don't go back") is the one with actual data behind it; the others are personal.
- **Ogilvy 1970s typing pool** [09:08]: Typewriting as professional signalling that *reduced* productivity but kept communication volume low — genuinely supports the signalling-vs-utility distinction and the email-externality point (burden shifts from sender to recipient).
- **AO delivery teddy bear** [23:17]: Trivial cost, high un-measurable long-term effect; the archetypal "you just have to make a subjective judgment" case. Strong illustration, unfalsifiable as evidence.
- **Amazon Prime / Prime Video / AWS internally hated except by Bezos** [24:17]: Supports two-way-door probabilistic thinking; well-known, credible.
- **Jaguar rebrand** [27:17–35:28]: The centrepiece extended case. Jaguar openly expects to lose "all but 15%" of customers, moving upmarket on an EV platform. Sutherland reframes it via innovator's dilemma (small ~60k/yr maker *can* pivot where volume makers can't), Dylan-goes-electric ("Judas") analogy, and Radiohead *Kid A* / Beatles *Sgt Pepper* analogies. He steelmans it ("understand what they're trying to do before you criticise") while conceding the ad was "deeply weird" and "a debranding exercise." Honest, but the analogies flatter the decision more than the evidence yet warrants.
- **Unlit motorway service station (Welsh borders)** [42:34–45:36]: His flagship opportunity-cost parable. Lion-bar-theft vs. unlit-forecourt asymmetry. Persuasive precisely because the numbers are unmeasurable — which is also its epistemic weakness.
- **Doorman vs automatic door** [39:30–42:34]: Consulting "gain-sharing" trick of defining a role by its automatable function then claiming the savings; ignores recognition, security, taxi-hailing, status. Roger Martin: "any idiot can cut costs." Strong illustration of subtraction-blindness.
- **London City Airport vs Schiphol/Changi/Dubai** [1:42:40]: Everyone optimised airports into shopping centres; City Airport "changed the rules of the game" with almost no shops and 5-minute boarding. Good evidence for his "pursue the marketplace, not a proxy metric" and rule-change points.
- **Moxy Hotel** [1:43:40]: Strips room service/laundry but gives a WeWork-like lobby you can linger in post-checkout — a smart re-triangulation of value. Supports multi-objective / customer-experience argument.
- **Coke "Hilltop" / "I'd Like to Teach the World to Sing" conceived during a flight delay at Dublin Airport** [1:50:46]: Shared adversity bonds people; supports "take people on a trip, don't rely on the office" for bonding. Anecdote, likely apocryphal-adjacent but illustrative.
- **Carlsberg "you know who your mates are" campaign** [1:51:47]: Getting drunk as a loyalty stress-test; ties to banter-as-signalling.
- **Cookie-banner law / cobra effect / Athens number plates** [1:52:47]: Regulatory own-goals; supports "give it to nudge theorists not lawyers."
- **Keynes as bursar of King's College — gave £1m to the Arts Theatre, nothing to the famous Chapel** [1:56:50]: Brilliant hypothecation case — fund what *can't* raise money elsewhere. Genuinely supports the allocation-intelligence point.
- **Ocado / Frozen Paratha from "Cinnamon Valley" (Wembley)** [1:35:32–1:37:34]: British under-celebration of excellence; winner-take-all "gray squirrel" homogenisation (sourdough, IPA) eliminating indigenous variety.
- **Dubai / Portugal Nomad visas / Italy €100k flat tax / UK millionaire exits** [1:06:56–1:10:01]: Evidence for tax-competition and "where wealth is earned vs spent" detachment. Data cited (UK 2nd in millionaire exits 2024, ~10,000) is used to argue policy, verges on advocacy.

## 5. Quotable lines

- "We're too impatient to be intelligent… intelligence and wisdom is slow whereas seeming logic is fast." [40:30–41:33]
- "Any idiot can cut costs. The real skill comes in cutting costs without actually losing long-term revenue as a consequence… short-term cost cutting is dangerously easy." [40:30] (crediting Roger Martin)
- "Sins of omission… dogs that don't bark in the night are much much less easy to identify than sins of commission, and we correspondingly get much less upset by them." [44:36]
- "Every spreadsheet is in some ways an act of pretense because it's past information which you pretend has wonderful predictive value… but it really doesn't, cuz weird [stuff] happens out of nowhere all the time." [25:17–26:17]
- "To economists prices are a number, but to consumers prices are a feeling." [2:03:53]
- "We think we know what we want but we don't… the way we discover what we want is by a kind of exploratory process of discovery." [15:13]

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Faster is not better.** *Orthodox:* immediate, free communication is an unalloyed good. *Inversion:* "Do we need slow AI, slow Tinder, slow Rightmove?" [14:12] — friction can protect quality (typewriting kept email-spam-style volume down; search that's too efficient hides value). *Steelman objection:* the friction he romanticises (typing pools) was pure deadweight loss; the paratha and downloads he loves are triumphs of *reduced* friction — he selectively valorises friction only where it flatters his point.
- **Efficiency is often the enemy.** *Orthodox:* cutting costs/optimising is prudent management. *Inversion:* "Nothing's more wasteful than to see something done efficiently that shouldn't be done at all" (Drucker); Musk should be director of *effectiveness* not efficiency [46:36]. *Steelman:* without measurable efficiency discipline, "un-measurable brand value" becomes an infinite excuse for any spend — his framework can't tell a valuable teddy bear from a wasteful one except after the fact.
- **Acquisition metrics mislead; loyalty is undervalued because it's slow to prove** [21:14–22:17]. *Steelman:* five-year unfalsifiable payback horizons are exactly where corporate slush and unaccountability hide.
- **Jaguar's "lose 85% of customers" rebrand is defensible, even smart** [27:17]. *Orthodox:* protect your base, "any publicity is good publicity is false." *Inversion:* for a sub-scale maker facing EV disruption, a Dylan-goes-electric bet-the-farm pivot may be rational. *Steelman:* survivorship bias — we cite Dylan and Sgt Pepper because they worked; countless alienating rebrands simply died.
- **Rich people should get privileges for paying more tax** (drive in bus lanes; IRS "consultant"; charitable queue-jumping) [1:27:21, 1:32:30, 1:57:50]. *Orthodox:* tax is a flat civic duty; buying privilege is corrupt. *Inversion:* people pay happily when status/framing/gratitude is attached ("thank you" text; hypothecation). *Steelman:* institutionalising pay-for-privilege corrodes the equal-citizenship logic of taxation and could entrench the very inequality he elsewhere warns is "bad for everybody."
- **Pubs, cafes, restaurants shouldn't pay rates/taxes** [1:10:01, 1:31:28] because they're social goods where the rich subsidise the poor's cheap drinks. Contrarian public-finance claim; steelman: arbitrary carve-outs invite lobbying and the cobra-effect gaming he elsewhere decries.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Defend the un-measurable touchpoints (doorman, delivery teddy bear, forecourt lights) against ROI-driven subtraction; frame them as insurance against slow, invisible revenue loss.
- Rebalance budget from short-term "performance" advertising toward long-term brand building, acknowledging the payback is real but slow to prove.
- Pursue multiple objectives (customers, employees, society, shareholders) rather than a single metric — long-lived companies (Nestlé, Unilever, P&G, Reckitt) keep a large marketing function that keeps them "rooted in the real but changing world of what customers want" [1:41:39].
- "Change the rules of the game" (London City Airport, Moxy) rather than out-optimising rivals on the incumbent metric.

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Design for *preference discovery*, not just filtering — over-efficient search (Rightmove) hides value and mis-serves users who don't yet know what they want.
- Price psychologically: avoid dead-zone anchors (list at £810k or £890k, not £850k) [16:13]; the "Amazon Prime" pattern (pay-once/annual) fixes the injustice of per-use charges that bankrupt heavy users (ULEZ, motorway tolls) [2:02:52].
- Segment on willingness-to-pay via **charitable yield management** — pay-to-skip queues/parking/lanes where the premium goes to charity, capturing "area under the curve" without resentment [1:57:50–2:00:52].
- Treat some big bets as two-way doors: cheap reversible experiments beat arguing; "the trial cost less than we'd have spent arguing about it" [25:17].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Add gratitude/framing: a "thank you" text for on-time payment beats penalties [1:28:23]; reframe costs as reciprocity ("we noticed, thank you") rather than duty.
- Use shared adversity/trips (not offices) to bond teams; ring-fence rent savings for "staff jollies" [1:49:46].
- For knowledge work, grant autonomy over environment ("50% library, 50% pub"); allow the midday break / light exposure that boosts real productivity [10:08–12:11, 1:47:44].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **"The measurable crowds out the important"** — likely the spine of *Alchemy* and recurs across the series (psycho-logic vs logic).
- **Signalling as the hidden engine of consumption** (luxury, early tech, typewriters, doormen) — expect in every Sutherland talk; ties to Veblen/Zahavi.
- **Opportunity cost / sins of omission invisibility** — a portable Sutherland module (Lion bar vs forecourt).
- **Probabilistic vs deterministic thinking / spreadsheet-as-pretence** — pairs with any talk on forecasting, Taleb, entrepreneurship.
- **Preference is discovered, not pre-specified** — recurs in dating, housing, decision architecture discussions.
- **Representative-agent blindness to inequality (ergodicity)** — Gary Stevenson / Ole Peters thread; reappears wherever averages mislead.
- **Winner-take-all homogenisation ("gray squirrel": sourdough, IPA)** — cultural-variety-loss theme.
- **"Built environment hasn't caught up with technology"** — remote work, trains-without-tables, airport pods; a standing Sutherland grievance.
- **Behavioural-economics-as-diplomacy** — nudges, hypothecation, charitable friction as humane policy design (Ariely, Thaler).

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where it identifies a *specific mechanism*: preference discovery defeating over-efficient search (the Rightmove "holes" and price-anchor dead zones), the asymmetry between measurable sins of commission and invisible sins of omission, and charitable yield management as a genuinely implementable idea that dissolves resentment while sorting by willingness-to-pay. These are testable and partly evidenced. It is weakest, and effectively unfalsifiable, where it rests on invented magnitudes ("£200,000 that night") and single anecdotes generalised to systemic law, and where the "un-measurable value" defence becomes an all-purpose licence: his framework cheers the AO teddy bear and the doorman but offers no principled way, ex ante, to distinguish a valuable un-measurable from a wasteful one — the same "subjective judgment" could bless almost any pet expense. His analogical method (Dylan, Sgt Pepper, dinosaur wings, gray squirrels) is persuasive theatre that smuggles in survivorship bias, and he half-admits it ("evolutionary psychology jumps the shark," "any publicity is good publicity is not true"). What would change the picture: controlled evidence that slow-feedback investments (loyalty, brand, retained un-automated roles) actually outperform their efficient substitutes on long horizons, rather than a curated highlight reel of cases where they happened to. Until then his thesis is best read as a corrective heuristic — a thumb on the scale against premature optimisation — rather than a decision rule.
