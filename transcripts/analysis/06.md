# 06. Why Logical Thinking is Illogical - Rory Sutherland

## 1. Core thesis
The problems worth solving are precisely the ones that resist logic: because "there's no shortage of logical people kicking around," any problem with a rational answer has already been solved, so what remains requires "something counterintuitive oblique tangential weird" [1:33:11]. Applied across the talk, this means the modern fetish for efficiency, optimisation, and the elimination of the human — in AI, economics, property, and protest — systematically destroys value that only irrational, human, or "inefficient" arrangements can create. Sutherland's throughline: economics and technology model humans as inert chess pieces, and almost every large social pathology (especially the property market) flows from that single false premise.

## 2. Argument structure
The interview is a rambling, associative dialogue rather than a single deductive spine, but a reconstruction runs:

- **Premise:** Conversation, trust, and the benefit of the doubt are ancient human technologies ("conversation and dogs are the two things that are really old" [00:00]) that machines and formal systems cannot replicate.
- **Evidence (anecdote):** The crying non-English-speaking woman on the wrong train, saved by a conductor who scrawls an instruction on the back of her ticket — "even if a computer could do that... we program not to do it because the benefit of the doubt would show up as a loss financially" [27:17].
- **Inference:** Efficiency-maximising systems strip out exactly the human latitude (trust, wiggle room, defaulting to trust) that makes them humane, because "the biggest cost is the wage cost" [13:08].
- **Extension by analogy:** He then transposes this same logic onto economics — the "single representative agent" and utility-maximiser assumptions [52:34] — and onto the property market, his central worked example.
- **Conclusion:** Since neoliberal models consider "an incredibly limited number of variables" [1:29:09], and since the interesting solutions are counterintuitive two-way doors [1:32:10], we should experiment (Georgism, land-value tax, front-loaded tax allowances, nomadic housing) rather than reason from first principles.

The reasoning is overwhelmingly **by analogy and anecdote**, almost never by data. The strongest analytic core — the georgist claim that land is categorically different from capital and its gains are unearned rent — is genuinely a live economic argument (Smith, George, Friedman are correctly invoked). But the leaps from "a conductor showed kindness" to "computers can never do this" and from "I dislike protest" to "protest is counterproductive survivorship bias" are rhetorical, not demonstrated. The property-market argument is his most rigorous; the AI and protest sections are suggestive assertion.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Costly signalling / runaway signalling (Zahavi's handicap principle; social identity theory):** His "purity spiral where people in groups adopt more and more extreme positions and behaviors to Signal their adherance to the group" [37:24] — "it's not enough to be averagely concerned... you have to go to more and more extreme behaviors" [38:24]. He twists signalling from the usual costly-honest-signal frame into a pathology of collective escalation.
- **Survivorship bias:** "there's massive survivorship bias in our belief in protests" [38:24] — we remember successful movements and forget the failed ones (CND "went nowhere" [38:24]).
- **System 1 / System 2 (Kahneman & Tversky):** "if you drive frequently driving becom a system one and it's really enjoyable" [1:38:14]; infrequent drivers never automate it, so it stays terrifying.
- **Single representative agent / Homo economicus (neoclassical economics, critiqued):** "you construct this kind of imaginary being who is kind of average of everybody... you assume that what's optimal for the average is optimal for everybody so you have a model which... can't really clock inequality" [52:34].
- **The "man of system" / humans as chess pieces (Adam Smith, *Theory of Moral Sentiments*):** economics behaves "as though humans were... chess pie chess pieces... each chest piece had its own individual will" [48:30] — Smith "warns against this precise thing."
- **Rent-seeking (classical/public-choice economics):** land ownership, patent trolling, and airport retail as extraction of value from owning a bottleneck without contributing work [1:06:45]–[1:07:48].
- **Georgism / land-value tax (Henry George):** the talk's spine — "socialism in relation to land... free market capitalism in relation to everything else" [1:07:48].
- **Demurrage / stamp scrip — money with an expiry (Silvio Gesell, whom Rory miscalls "an Italian socialist"):** money "should basically have an expiration date... to encourage people to spend it rather than hoarding it up" [50:32], boosting the **velocity of money**.
- **Multiple private currencies (Hayek):** airline air miles "are exactly what Hayek talked about when he talked about multiple currencies" [51:33].
- **Loss aversion / endowment effect / status-quo bias (Kahneman, Thaler):** the "irrational attachment" of the old to houses, "terror of downsizing," "loss of status" [1:35:12]; "I respect irrational attachment" [1:12:57].
- **FOMO / herd behaviour:** reluctance to leave London is "basically a massive Collective fomo" [59:40].
- **Speculative bubble / positional (Veblen) goods:** the Dutch tulip mania where "Rising prices brought in ever more demand" [1:03:44], and property as "purely rivalrous" [1:03:44] consumption.
- **Division of labour / comparative advantage (Adam Smith):** the "info Butler"/"Uber Jeeves" AI intermediary as "a bit from Adam Smith... division of labor" [13:08]; the **centaur** model — "the best chess player is a human plus a machine" [12:08].
- **The alignment problem (AI safety):** Google Maps and rail-booking algorithms "the algorithm doesn't really know what you're trying to do" [20:11].
- **Choice architecture / defaults (Thaler & Sunstein):** a machine that "switches it from ABC to querty" or infers Liverpool Lime Street from "Liverpool Street Station" [24:15].
- **Two-way doors / reversibility (Jeff Bezos):** "it's actually easier to try it out in reality than it is to argue it in theory" [1:32:10].
- **Obliquity (John Kay):** goals best reached indirectly — he name-checks the book explicitly [1:33:11].
- **The adjacent possible (Stuart Kauffman):** "in evolutionary terms for electric cars the adjacent possible is much bigger" [1:47:24].
- **Trust as transaction-cost reduction (institutional economics, à la Fukuyama):** Quaker "your word is your bond" meant business "didn't really require any lawyers or any of these tedious trust grating intermediaries" [21:12].

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **Socrates/Plato dialogues as "simulacrum" of conversation** [01:01]–[02:02]: writing philosophy down is a lossy transcription of the ideal (live dialectic). Evidence FOR his opening claim that conversation is the primal, richest medium; largely rhetorical scene-setting, but genuinely frames the AI-market-research point.
- **AI market research** [02:02]–[03:03]: AI lets you "start an inquiry without preconceptions as to the end point," unlike questionnaires that "reflect a preconception." Supports the value of open-ended discovery; a fair, if unquantified, claim.
- **Amazon Alexa** [09:08]: several billion spent, but 90% of queries are "what time is it, set an alarm, what's the weather" — "it would have been cheaper... to give everybody an egg timer and a barometer." Evidence FOR interface-mismatch; a genuinely sharp illustration that voice-without-screen was the wrong bet.
- **The "info Butler"/"Uber Jeeves" AI intermediary** [10:08]–[13:08]: a human expert serving ~100 clients at £100/month. Evidence FOR bringing back "domestic Service as a really lucrative form of employment" via division of labour. Speculative business idea, doing thematic work (human-in-the-loop beats disintermediation).
- **Manned railway ticket offices / split ticketing / Trainline** [14:08]–[19:10]: he campaigned to keep human ticket sellers because a trusted human can say "you need the off Peak return" or sell a peak single plus off-peak return to save £9. The Waterloo-via-Salisbury cheap-train hack [18:10]–[19:10] shows algorithms "overweight the importance of Journey time." Strong evidence FOR human judgment and the alignment problem; concrete and checkable.
- **Google Maps public-transport bias** [16:09]–[18:10]: "very Californian" assumption that public-transport users have no car, so it never models drive-to-station-then-train. Evidence FOR baked-in cultural assumptions in "objective" tools. Persuasive, drawn from *Alchemy*.
- **The Quakers** [20:11]–[22:13]: tiny population founding Cadbury, Rowntree, Barclays, Lloyds; barred from universities so forced into trade; "your word is your bond" cut out lawyers. Evidence FOR trust as an efficiency engine. Genuinely supports the claim, though the "barred from Oxbridge" causation is a partial story.
- **The crying woman on the wrong train** [25:15]–[27:17]: conductor writes instructions on her ticket. His emotional centrepiece for "the benefit of the doubt... would show up as a loss financially" [27:17]. Vivid, but a single anecdote carrying a universal claim about machine limits.
- **Chess computers and "creativity"** [23:15]–[24:15]: once "impossible" queen sacrifices are now routine — "creativity" collapses into "highly refined pattern recognition." Interestingly, this **undercuts** his own machine-can't-do-trust argument, which he half-concedes.
- **Peter Hitchens walking out** [28:17]–[29:18]: (Alex's anecdote, endorsed by Rory) illustrating hyper-rationality/temperament; colour, not argument.
- **Neil Kinnock's 1992 Sheffield rally + CND clown/skeleton-suit protests** [37:24]–[39:25]: performative signalling that repels the broadcast audience — "Winston Churchill... it was not necessary for him to also dress up in a skeleton suit" [39:25]. Evidence FOR protest-as-counterproductive-signalling. Cherry-picked; ignores successful mass movements.
- **London media bias toward demonstrations** [40:25]: "the people of Liverpool would have to burn the city to the ground to get the same media attention as... 50,000 londoners." Evidence FOR geographic distortion of the "public opinion" signal.
- **Jordan Peterson moving talks to the morning** [42:28]–[44:29]: protests "disappeared overnight" because "people couldn't be bothered to get out of bed" — proof (he says) that protest has become "a form of entertainment," "Tinder for lefties" [40:25]. Second-hand, self-admittedly possibly exaggerated, but rhetorically potent.
- **The Hindu temple altar (elephant, monkey, Jesus)** [50:32]: his friend "jug Bala"'s mother sees no contradiction — a critique of Western "monotheism" of explanation, the demand for "one Theory to explain everything." Analogy for epistemic pluralism.
- **Silvio Gesell's expiring money vs Sainsbury's vouchers** [50:32]–[51:33]: marketers already invented demurrage — the "£5 off if you spend £60, expires in two weeks" voucher drives spending velocity. Nice bridge from fringe economics to everyday marketing.
- **Roger Martin's front-loaded tax allowance** [53:35]–[54:35]: first CA$100–200k of *lifetime* earnings tax-free, so people accumulate wealth "early, which is when you need it." Evidence FOR the claim that the tax system is perversely timed against the young.
- **The Harvester widow / the £2.2m house / bed-blockers** [57:38]–[58:39]: asset-rich pensioners "worried about the price of lemons" while their children can't fix car shock absorbers; retirees are "effectively bed blockers for the property Market" [58:39]. The talk's emotional engine for taxing wealth over income. Powerful but anecdotal.
- **Buying new cars / new iPhones as philanthropy** [1:00:43]–[1:01:44]: the new-buyer eats depreciation so a poorer person gets a nearly-as-good six-year-old Jag or three-year-old iPhone cheaply. Evidence FOR "consumption has positive externalities" — clever inversion, though it ignores manufacturing/environmental externalities (which he waves at re jet skis).
- **Dutch tulip mania** [1:02:44]–[1:03:44]: contrasted with property — "I don't have to buy a tulip... but you need housing." Evidence FOR property being uniquely coercive rivalrous consumption.
- **The double-income household trap** [1:19:02]–[1:22:05]: starts as option, becomes obligation; gains "soaked up in the shape of higher property prices"; households lost "35 hours of discretionary time every week." Cites Elizabeth Warren and Fay Weldon as sources. His most sociologically substantive case.
- **Caribbean chattel houses / US trailer parks / Irish Traveller nomadism** [1:18:01]–[1:19:02], [1:24:07]–[1:25:08], [1:44:23]: house-ownership decoupled from land-ownership as the historical/practical solution. Evidence FOR his land-vs-capital distinction; genuinely illustrative.
- **Nuclear power stations on ships** [1:27:09]–[1:28:09]: temporary, mobile infrastructure defuses NIMBY objection ("the thing's going to be in Harbor for eight months and then it's going to go somewhere else"). Applies the reversibility/two-way-door idea to planning politics.
- **HS2 funded by self-awarded planning permission** [1:30:09]–[1:32:10]: buy agricultural land (~£3–4k/acre), build the railway, grant yourself planning permission (land jumps to ~£1m+/acre), capture the uplift. Evidence FOR land-value capture; his single most concrete policy proposal. Kent County Council allegedly proposed selling planning permission and "the treasury just had a complete... contion" [1:32:10].
- **Elon Musk / Tesla, RVs, Citroën Ami, cargo bikes, micromobility** [1:40:15]–[1:47:24]: Starlink + batteries + solar + EVs enable nomadism that could "destroy the whole property market." Speculative capstone tying tech optimism to the property thesis.

## 5. Quotable lines

- "test counterintuitive things because all... the logical things have been tried already... if there were a logical answer to your problem some would... have already solved it... so by a kind of logic Darwinism you can say that the problems that... submit to logic have mostly gone away and we're left for the ones that require something counterintuitive oblique tangential weird" [1:33:11] — the title thesis in his own words.
- "we have a weird Tech Fetish around the optimization of efficiency which basically means the elimination of humanity from every process because the biggest cost is the wage cost" [13:08].
- "the benefit of the doubt would show up as a loss financially... so the first thing to be closed down is the benefit of the doubt" [27:17].
- "I'm not really a capitalist I'm a consumerist" [52:34]; and its partner: "why is saving better than spending it's just consumerism needlessly postponed" [55:36].
- "property ownership is not wealth it's consumption you're consuming the property in which you live by depriving someone else of the right to live there" [1:34:12].
- "it's Tinder for lefties" [40:25] — on modern protest.

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Disintermediation is good → keep the human middleman.** Orthodoxy: technology should remove costly human intermediaries (estate agents, ticket clerks). His inversion: pay a trusted human "info Quaker" precisely because "I want a transaction of that scale to be mediated by a human being" [14:08]. Steelman objection: he ignores that intermediaries also extract rent and gatekeep; the crying-woman anecdote proves humans *can* be kind, not that machines *can't* learn to be, which his own chess-creativity point concedes.
- **Property price rises are good news → they are bad news for almost everyone.** Orthodoxy: rising house prices are prosperity. His inversion: they are "good news for an extraordinarily small group... other than that it's bad news for everybody else" [1:18:01], and property is consumption, not investment. Steelman: housing wealth funds pensions, care, and downstream inheritance for the majority who do own; his "rivalrous consumption" framing understates that supply constraints, not the concept of ownership, drive scarcity.
- **Tax income → tax land/wealth (Georgism).** Orthodoxy: tax productive income. His inversion: "taxing wealth... seems healthier to me than taxing income" [55:36] because land gains are unearned rent. Steelman: land-value tax is theoretically elegant but has famous practical problems (illiquid asset-rich/cash-poor owners, valuation disputes, one-off transition shocks) — the very Harvester widow he mocks is the standard objection to LVT, which he brushes aside as "georgism killed" by one newspaper story [1:12:57].
- **Save/hoard → spend fast (demurrage, consumerism).** Orthodoxy: saving is prudent and virtuous. His inversion: hoarded wealth "means you either don't need the money or lack the imagination to spend it" [55:36]; money should circulate. Steelman: velocity-boosting is inflationary and ignores precautionary and investment functions of saving.
- **Protest advances causes → protest sets them back.** Orthodoxy: demonstrations are democratic pressure. His inversion: runaway signalling and survivorship bias make most protest counterproductive theatre [37:24]–[44:29]. Steelman: heavy selection bias in his examples; civil-rights, suffrage, and independence movements are the obvious counter-cases he doesn't engage.
- **The old deserve deference → not this cohort.** Orthodoxy: respect your elders. His inversion: today's asset-rich boomers "didn't fight in World War II... they're crap" [1:13:59] and shouldn't be venerated like the wartime generation. Steelman: deliberately provocative, ethically thin, and conflates a policy point (intergenerational tax fairness) with a cultural insult.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Trust is a marketable efficiency: brands that credibly signal total trustworthiness (the Quaker "word is your bond" reputation the Quaker Oats company *borrowed* without being Quakers [20:11]) cut the buyer's transaction costs and command premiums.
- Build expiry and velocity into loyalty mechanics — the Sainsbury's "£5 off if you spend £60, expires in two weeks" voucher [50:32] is applied demurrage; scarcity-of-time drives action better than discount size.
- Sell the counterintuitive: "the way to sell more products might be to put the price up rather than taking it down" [30:19] — price as a quality/status signal.

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Match interface to task, don't fetishise one channel — Alexa's screenless bet failed because voice suits few queries [09:08]; think multimodal.
- Design the human-in-the-loop deliberately (the centaur: "the best chess player is a human plus a machine" [12:08]) rather than optimising the human out.
- Decouple bundled value propositions: property's core defect is conflating "a place to live" with "an investment in land" [1:44:23]; any product that forces customers to buy an unwanted investment alongside a needed good is ripe for disruption (chattel houses, trailer parks, Citroën Ami rental).
- Preserve "benefit of the doubt" and wiggle room in automated service flows; pure rule-codification "manifests itself as bureaucracy" and destroys goodwill [24:15]–[27:17].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Prefer reversible experiments ("two-way doors" [1:32:10]) — argue less, test more; it disarms opposition faster than theory.
- Reduce the *cost of compliance*, don't just amplify the message — Peterson defeated protest by moving talks to the morning [42:28], i.e., change the friction, not the argument.
- Read a person's whole "feed" before judging: the Twitter test — insane volume + all-politics = "in the grip of a kind of mental... derangement," but "a few pictures of their kittens" means they're worth engaging [46:30].

## 8. Cross-reference hooks

- **Psycho-logic over logic** — "counterintuitive, oblique, tangential" solutions is the *Alchemy* master-theme; this whole talk is its applied edition.
- **Signalling / costly signals** — recurs anywhere he discusses brands, protest, status, and Veblen goods; here as "runaway signalling" and property as positional consumption.
- **The Google Maps / alignment critique** — explicitly from *Alchemy*; watch for other "objective tool encodes a hidden assumption" instances.
- **Economics-as-pseudoscience** — "all forms of Economics is utter bollocks apart from the austrians" [48:30]; his standing hostility to Homo economicus and the representative agent.
- **Trust and the loss of the human** — Quakers, conductors, estate agents; likely to reappear as "the value of inefficiency."
- **Adam Smith as ally, not libertarian mascot** — Smith on inheritance tax, ground rent, and the "man of system"; a recurring reclamation move.
- **The adjacent possible / evolutionary framing of innovation** — miniaturisation of EVs; expect this Kauffman-derived lens elsewhere.
- **Georgism / land-value capture** — a distinctive hobby-horse that will likely surface again in policy discussions.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is **strongest** on the property market and land-value tax: here Sutherland has a genuine analytic core (Smith's and George's distinction between capital and land, the unearned-rent critique, quantifiable illustrations like HS2 land uplift and the double-income time cost) and it is falsifiable — you could run the Kent planning-permission experiment or a georgist pilot and measure results, which is exactly his "two-way doors" prescription. It is **weakest and most unfalsifiable** where it leans on temperament dressed as principle: the claims that machines can *never* extend the benefit of the doubt (undercut by his own admission that chess "creativity" was just pattern recognition), that protest is *inherently* counterproductive (pure survivorship-bias selection, ignoring every successful mass movement), and that the elderly asset-rich "deserve" less — an ethical assertion smuggled in as economics. His signature rhetorical move — a single vivid anecdote (the crying woman, the Harvester widow) carrying a universal law — is persuasive but epistemically fragile. What would change the picture: actual data on land-value-tax pilots (transition shocks, valuation feasibility, effects on the illiquid elderly), evidence on whether AI systems *can* be tuned to grant latitude without runaway fraud, and any rigorous protest-outcome study showing selection effects cut the other way. As so often with Sutherland, the value is not in whether each claim is *true* but in the reframes he licenses — property as consumption, saving as postponed consumerism, efficiency as humanity-elimination — which are cheap to test and expensive to have ignored.
