# 21. $22,381 Worth of Marketing Advice in 63 Minutes

## 1. Core thesis
Marketing is not a measurable line-item cost but a holistic multiplier on every other business process, and the modern obsession with ROI and quantification systematically destroys value by rewarding what reveals itself quickly (cost-cutting, cheap-channel customer acquisition) over what reveals itself slowly (customer retention, fame, brand). Because human demand is created rather than pre-existing, and because buyers reason psycho-logically rather than economically, the marketer's real job is "knowing what economists are wrong about" — and the biggest gains for small businesses come from removing invisible signals that say "not for you / not open" rather than from clever campaigns.

## 2. Argument structure
- **Premise:** Marketing exists to create behavioral change, and that change is often achieved obliquely (e.g. by becoming famous so customers come to you — reactive beats proactive selling). [00:00–01:01]
- **Premise:** Marketers suffer "logistics envy / procurement envy" — they crave the spreadsheet-provable legitimacy of other departments, so they embraced ROI accountability sold to them by the "tech-consulting-finance industrial complex." [01:01–02:01]
- **Inference:** But "you can measure what you do; you should not assume that what you measure is the sum value of what you're doing." Making measurability the criterion of permission blocks almost-certainly-profitable activity. [03:02]
- **Evidence (by anecdote/analogy, not data):** Underinvestment in customer service (bank customers go "inert" over 5 years); the AO cuddly bears whose ROI would take 7 years to prove; Amazon's "call me back" button; the unlit motorway service station; the unanswered fish-and-chip phone; the coffee-shop 3:30 demand illusion; the John Lewis Tunbridge Wells post-mortem.
- **Inference:** Data is chronologically and demographically biased — "cost-saving data appears fast, value-creating data appears slow" — so correcting for it must sometimes be subjective. [09:06–10:07]
- **Second track (economics is wrong about demand):** Economics assumes pre-existing demand satisfied at lowest transaction cost; in reality demand is created, and people frequently want the *opposite* of streamlined, cheap transactions (farmers' markets, expensive wine, human ticket conversations). [21:19–24:20]
- **Conclusion:** Therefore competent small-business marketing is mostly the "180° flip" — seeing your business as an ignorant customer would — and stopping the unconscious signals that repel people. Get every small business 30% better and you add 2–3% to GDP. [15:11, 19:15]

The reasoning is overwhelmingly by analogy and vivid anecdote. The leap from single stories to general laws (e.g. one coffee shop → all coffee-shop demand data is confounded) is rhetorically powerful but empirically unforced; the strength is that the mechanism (system-one perception, signalling) is independently plausible.

## 3. Named psychological principles
- **Goodhart's Law** (explicitly named twice): "any metric that becomes a target loses its value as a metric." Applied to ROI-driven marketing and to staff-pay-cutoff incentives. Origin: Charles Goodhart. [05:04, 45:35–46:36]
- **Salience of losses vs. opportunity costs / omission bias:** "the sin of omission in marketing gets much less attention... opportunity costs are much less salient than costs" — the stolen Lion bar vs. the unlit forecourt. Kin to loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky) but Sutherland's own twist about visible vs. invisible loss. [17:13–18:14]
- **Costly signalling / conspicuous consumption:** Champagne's price is the message — it reliably signals importance, generosity, hospitality *because* it can't be bought cheaply. Roots in Veblen (conspicuous consumption) and Zahavi/Spence (handicap principle, signalling). [24:20–26:20]
- **Second-order intelligence / theory of mind → the "too good to be true" heuristic:** buyers infer quality from price ("you get what you pay for"), so a high-spec, low-price product creates cognitive dissonance and non-purchase (Nespresso Virtuo/Virtuo Plus, frozen food). Cognitive dissonance origin: Festinger. [26:20–29:22]
- **IKEA effect:** Charlie Bigham's dishes that must be oven-cooked; added effort raises perceived value. Origin: Norton, Mochon & Ariely. [33:27–34:27]
- **System 1 / automatic perception:** chairs outside a cafe, lights in a window read by the amygdala without conscious reasoning. Kahneman's dual-process framing. [19:15, 34:27, 37:30]
- **Confounding variables / representativeness of data:** the coffee-demand-after-3:30 illusion; "the first lesson of statistics is ask to what extent the data is representative." Gigerenzer-adjacent statistical-reasoning critique. [10:07, 45:35]
- **Jack-of-all-trades / specialist heuristic:** consumers infer competence from focus (fishmonger, butcher, oyster stall, Gordon's gin "you can only be really good at one thing"). Sutherland's own coinage; ties to signalling. [54:36–56:38]
- **Choice architecture / focus:** Apple and McDonald's rescued by radical product reduction; the iPhone 5C failure. Thaler & Sunstein. [1:00:39–1:02:40]
- **The magician / authority as a suspension of disbelief:** Steve Jobs and Elon Musk let audiences suspend cynicism (per a conversation with Dan Ariely). Cialdini's authority, twisted into "personal mythology." [33:27]
- **Social proof:** empty restaurants stay empty; Dishoom opening for breakfast to populate the room; "nobody wants to be the only customer." Cialdini. [47:36–48:36]
- **Complex systems / non-linearity:** "once you've acknowledged human perception... you can't afford to be reductionist, linear, purely mathematical — you're in complex-system space." [12:09]
- **Psycho-logic / "opposite of a good idea is another good idea":** Bohr/Einstein analogy applied to markets — streamlined M&S self-scan AND farmers' markets both win. A signature Sutherland principle. [22:20–23:20]

## 4. Case studies & examples
- **High Street banks / customer service:** bad service makes customers "inert" not lost, taking ~5 years to show; firms therefore over-invest in acquisition. Evidence FOR: slow-revealing retention value. Genuinely supports the measurability-bias claim. [04:03]
- **Amazon "call me back" button & "does this benefit the customer first":** Amazon "tests everything," so smaller firms can steal proven innovations without their own business case. Supports "don't demand granular proof" — though it also concedes Amazon *did* measure, slightly undercutting the anti-measurement line. [10:07–12:09]
- **AO delivery cuddly bears:** ROI provable maybe 7 years out; not worth quantifying. Supports the "some potent cheap activities need no granular proof" claim; rhetorically strong, evidentially thin. [13:11]
- **Motorway service station on the A40 (unlit, looked like the Bates Motel):** opportunity cost of looking closed dwarfs pilferage but generates no anxiety. Central exhibit for omission-bias / the 180° flip. Strong illustrative work. [16:11–18:14]
- **Champagne vs. Prosecco / Chapel Down & British sparkling wine:** priced at ~£23+ to actually "do the job of champagne" (signal occasion/generosity); quality alone at £8.95 fails. Best case for costly signalling; persuasive. [24:20–26:20]
- **Nespresso Virtuo vs. Virtuo Plus (same price):** absence of a price gap broke the price-quality inference and confused buyers into buying neither. Direct evidence for the too-good-to-be-true / assumed-tradeoff mechanism. [28:22–29:22]
- **Frozen food & the NASA-pouch Indian food (Berkeley founders), Charlie Bigham, Cook, Picard:** superb product stigmatised by low price; needs the IKEA effect, craft packaging, or a "magician" frontman to overcome disbelief. Illustrates the psychological penalty of being too good and too cheap. [29:22–34:27]
- **Answering the phone / William Sainsbury's dying words ("keep the stores well lit"), corner-shop window posters blocking light, Busy Bites unlit entrance, blacked-out Covent Garden restaurant:** unconscious "we're shut/not for you" signals bleed revenue. Cumulative support for the 180° flip. [35:28–39:32]
- **The 3:30 coffee illusion / Grace coffee shop in Hong Kong (Mojitos experiment):** staff radiating closing-down cues (upturned chairs, mopping, pay cutoff at 4:15) suppress demand; the data blames time-of-day. The confounding-variable showpiece and a genuine natural experiment (they kept the machine on and coffee outsold mojitos). Strong. [43:34–47:36]
- **Dishoom opening for breakfast:** solves the empty-at-lunchtime social-proof problem by seeding bodies in the room. Good social-proof case. [47:36–48:36]
- **John Lewis at Home, Tunbridge Wells (post-mortem):** own car park, badly sited entrance/sign, narrow-edge signage making it look small, and the "at Home" name signalling furniture-only — a stack of self-inflicted "not for me" signals killed a Cornucopia store. The set-piece synthesis of the whole talk. [48:36–52:36]
- **Thrasher's fries (Ocean City), Five Guys, Gordon's gin, the imagined "bacon sandwich" chain, Euro­star breakfast:** "corporate assholery" / rule-bound focus signals confidence and quality (only salt & vinegar, no ketchup). Supports the specialist heuristic + signalling value of not being customer-focused. [56:38–59:39]
- **Apple (Jobs' cull to 4 products; iPhone 5C misstep) & McDonald brothers (car-manufacturing 90° flip from the American diner):** focus as rescue and the market gap at the opposite end. Supports choice-architecture and "opposite of a good idea." [1:00:39–1:02:40]
- **Enterprise Allowance Scheme (Thatcher era) & 1980s bands-as-businesses:** treating a band as a limited company induced real business discipline. Supports "give everyone a company" and the identity-shift argument; entertaining, tangential to marketing per se. [41:32–43:34]

## 6. Contrarian claims
- **ROI / measurement:** *Orthodox:* marketing must be quantified and justified by measurable return. *Inversion:* the most valuable marketing is neither measurable nor attributable, and demanding proof blocks it. *Steelman objection:* without measurement, budgets become unaccountable vanity spend; "fame is invaluable" is unfalsifiable cover for waste. Sutherland's own admission that Amazon *did* measure the call-me-back button weakens the absolutism.
- **Cheap = good:** *Orthodox:* lower price at equal quality always wins the customer. *Inversion:* people often want to pay more (wine, champagne, farmers' markets), and too-good-too-cheap triggers disbelief and non-purchase. *Steelman:* this holds for signalling/occasion goods and skeptical categories, but for the bulk of everyday commodity purchases price competition manifestly does drive share — he cherry-picks the categories where psycho-logic dominates.
- **Demand is pre-existing:** *Orthodox:* neoclassical utility maximisation. *Inversion:* demand is manufactured by being in the right place/time with the right message. *Steelman:* "created out of nowhere" overstates it; most successful marketing channels latent, not literally non-existent, demand.
- **Customer-centricity:** *Orthodox:* always maximise customer convenience and choice. *Inversion:* rigid, non-accommodating rules ("no ketchup," oven-only) signal confidence and quality and can raise appeal. *Steelman:* survivorship bias — Thrasher's and Five Guys succeeded, but countless inflexible businesses simply annoyed customers into leaving.

## 7. Practical applications
**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Do the "180° flip": audit your business nightly as an ignorant first-time customer would — signage, typography, lighting, entrance visibility. Fix the invisible "we're shut / not for you" signals first; they're cheaper and higher-leverage than campaigns.
- Keep the lights on and streaming outward; put chairs/tables out; answer the phone (and call back missed numbers) — behaviors, not words, that read as "open and keen."
- Pursue "fame" deliberately: reactive selling (customers come to you) beats proactive; visibility multiplies hiring, partnerships, and returned phone calls.

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Price signalling goods (wine, premium alternatives) high enough to "do the job" — a British sparkling wine must clear ~£23 to substitute for champagne regardless of blind-taste quality.
- Never leave two products at the same price when one is better; insert a deliberate price gap so the price-quality inference resolves (the Nespresso fix).
- To sell a product that's "too good to be true" (cheap-but-excellent, e.g. shelf-stable gourmet food), add friction/ritual (IKEA effect — oven not microwave), craft packaging, or a "magician" frontman to license belief.
- Focus the range: fewer SKUs signal expertise (specialist heuristic) and can rescue a business (Apple, McDonald's, Gordon's gin).

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- Seed social proof: never let a venue/offer look empty; populate the room (Dishoom breakfast; free/outdoor seating) because no one wants to be the first customer.
- Interrogate confounded data like a detective (obvious Adams / The Big Short): when a logical explanation stops your inquiry, look for the hidden variable (staff behavior, sightlines, naming) before acting.
- A little rule-bound "corporate assholery" ("take it or leave it") can signal confidence and quality more than accommodation.

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest where the psychological mechanism is independently well-established and the anecdote merely dramatises it: signalling (champagne), the price-quality inference (Nespresso), social proof (empty restaurants), and confounded operational data (the coffee illusion) are both plausible and, in the coffee case, semi-experimentally supported. It is weakest precisely where it is most rhetorically seductive — the wholesale dismissal of measurement. Sutherland repeatedly leans on "fame is invaluable but unquantifiable" and "you don't need the business case," which is close to unfalsifiable and could rationalise any unaccountable spend; tellingly, his best example (Amazon) succeeds *because* it measured. The generalisation from single vivid stories to universal laws is method-by-anecdote, vulnerable to survivorship bias (Thrasher's and Five Guys won; the inflexible losers aren't cited). And "demand created out of nowhere" overstates what is usually the channelling of latent demand. What would change the picture: controlled or quasi-experimental evidence on brand/fame spend and long-run retention (holdout markets, geo-experiments), base-rate data on how often "signal-fixing" interventions actually move revenue, and cases where rigid non-customer-centricity failed — which would tell us whether these are laws or merely well-told exceptions.
