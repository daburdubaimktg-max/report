# 32. Rory Sutherland Masterclass — "Selling Fast and Slow"

## 1. Core thesis
Modern business systematically disguises open-ended, creative questions as closed mathematical-optimization problems — not because that produces better answers, but because a "rational" model absolves the decision-maker of blame. Sutherland's central argument is that this fetish for efficiency, speed, and pre-rationalized business cases destroys value: the most important ideas, decisions, and human experiences require slowness, exploration, and effort, and organizations that optimize those away starve themselves like a beehive that fires its scout bees.

## 2. Argument structure
The talk is built as an extended analogy chain rather than a data argument.

- **Premise (opening puzzle):** Why are people "unappreciative of, verging on hostile to, creative solutions to problems?" [00:07–01:07]. He frames creativity as expanding the solution space and giving people "almost for free five different ways they could look at the problem" [01:07].
- **Central evidence-by-analogy (the bees):** 20% of bees ignore the waggle dance and forage at random. Evolution "hates wastage" yet tolerates them because of the **explore/exploit trade-off** [02:09–03:10]. Hives that only exploit get "trapped in a local maximum," can't get lucky, can't grow, and eventually go extinct [03:10–04:10]. Inference: the scout bees look like a cost through the eyes of an "obedient bee," but they are the whole growth mechanism.
- **Inference to business:** Two failure modes — (1) cutting explore to hit "your next quarterly target," and (2) the discovering bees "come back to the hive and no one fucking listens" [05:10–06:10]. He claims both happen in parallel in modern business.
- **Reinforcing premise (performative rationality):** Most business questions are "open-ended questions," not "high-school math questions," but effort is spent pretending the opposite to avoid blame — "performative rationality" and "unaccountability sins" [08:11–10:15].
- **Conclusion:** We conflate efficiency with effectiveness because "efficiency has easy maths and effectiveness has complicated maths" [22:29–23:30]. The remedy: license imagination, testing the opposite, and post-rationalization; "what if slower is better?" [31:37].

The reasoning is almost entirely analogy + anecdote (bees, HS2, wet trousers, Uber map, handwritten letters, the pesometer). There is no controlled data. The leap from bee ecology to corporate R&D budgeting is rhetorically powerful but assumes business "extinction" dynamics mirror evolutionary ones — it holds as an intuition pump, not as proof.

## 3. Named psychological principles

- **Explore/exploit trade-off (multi-armed bandit / optimization theory):** His organizing frame. Drawn explicitly from "computer algorithm design" [03:10]. He twists it by insisting it is "not a trade-off at all… it's a benign feedback loop" [04:10–05:10] — exploit only has value if you keep discovering.
- **Local maximum / over-optimization:** Hives "get trapped in a local maximum, they get over-optimized on the past" [03:10]. A hill-climbing metaphor for firms optimizing legacy KPIs.
- **Costly signalling (Zahavi; Spence in economics):** Named explicitly around handwritten direct mail and the £800 tap demo — "it's costly signalling of a kind, and this is the ultimate costly signalling" [21:29]. Effort/time/love invested in a message signals the importance of the underlying decision.
- **Psycho-logic / the opposite of a good idea is another good idea (Bohr, via Sutherland):** He credits Niels Bohr in conversation with Einstein — in high-complexity physics (and psychology) the opposite of a good idea can be another good idea [11:17]. His signature refutation of single-optimum thinking.
- **Uncertainty vs. duration in waiting (operations psychology / the Uber map effect):** "It's not actually the duration, it's actually not knowing" — people prefer a known 15-minute wait to an uncertain 6-minute one [13:18–14:19]. Reducing uncertainty beats reducing time.
- **Bass diffusion / habit & social proof (Cialdini for social proof; Frank Bass for diffusion):** New behaviours spread slowly because early on they have "neither the strength of habit… nor the strength of social proof" [18:23]. Applied to vaccination and rural electrification.
- **Framing effect (Kahneman & Tversky):** The speedometer-vs-"pesometer" demonstration — identical physical information (60 mph = 10 min per 10 miles) produces completely different behaviour depending on presentation [23:30–26:31]. "A physicist can't tell the difference; psychologically… it has completely different effects."
- **Goodhart's law (implicit):** Programmatic marketing lets "your readiness to produce high-speed attributable data" define customer value, displacing true lifetime value [16:20–17:22] — the metric becomes the target and corrupts the objective.
- **Opportunity cost (reframed as "creative opportunity cost"):** The cost of pretending questions are mathematical is "absolutely immense" [10:15].
- **Left-brain/right-brain division (McGilchrist, *The Master and His Emissary*):** The "Iain McGilchrist left-hemisphere one-way street" where right-brain ideas must be ratified by the left brain but never vice versa [30:36].
- **Neoliberal/mainstream economics as the villain discipline:** Accused of dishonestly reducing every problem to "a couple of mathematical factors, usually something like price" [09:14–10:15] — an implicit critique of von Mises-style rational-actor modelling.

## 4. Case studies & examples

- **The beehive / scout bees [02:09–05:10]:** 20% of bees forage randomly. Evidence FOR the explore/exploit thesis. Doing genuine analogical work — it's the load-bearing metaphor — but it is illustration, not evidence that firms behave like hives.
- **HS2 (High Speed 2 rail) [07:10–08:11]:** Engineers reduced the brief to speed and capacity. Sutherland contrasts with a hypothetical Disney brief: "how do you make the journey from London to Manchester by train so enjoyable people feel stupid taking the car" — slow "Harry Potter" trains, champagne, ball pits. Evidence FOR: closed metrics crowd out creative reframing. Rhetorical/hypothetical — Disney never bid; the counterfactual is unfalsifiable but vivid.
- **Transport for London commute research [14:19–16:20]:** An (unnamed) psychologist found people enjoy their commute (slight male skew — "men are like Sky boxes, they've got a standby mode"). Planners suppressed it because "all our models rely on the assumption that time spent in transit is a disutility." His strongest concrete example of decisions taken "for the mathematical tractability of the model" rather than human benefit.
- **Smallpox: Mary Wortley Montagu (variolation) & Edward Jenner (cowpox vaccine) [17:22–18:23]:** Both were marketing challenges, not just discoveries — Montagu won by converting the royal family; Jenner spent 15–20 years changing minds. Evidence FOR: "everything important takes fucking ages" and social-proof-driven diffusion.
- **Rural electrification of the British/Irish home [18:23–19:25]:** Took 30–40 years and an actual marketing budget. Evidence FOR the slowness of adoption.
- **Handwritten letters to buy an off-market house (and Impact, the semi-automation business) [19:25–21:29]:** A family wrote handwritten letters to owners of not-for-sale houses; several replied, three offered, they bought one. Impact's handwritten-mail response rates are "10, 15, 20 times higher" than other media, "a hundred times higher than digital." Evidence FOR costly signalling — effort perceived as commensurate with the decision's scale. His best commercial data point, though "astronomical" figures are asserted, not sourced.
- **The £800 tap with a 20-minute Zoom demo [21:29–22:29]:** Expensive, slow selling closed a sale he'd otherwise never have made (over his ex-procurement wife's objections). Evidence FOR: some valuable customers are slow/expensive to convert.
- **Programmatic marketing [16:20–17:22, 22:29]:** Optimizes for impulse buyers in low-cost channels, letting short-term convertibility define the customer base "and then we wonder why the economy isn't growing." Evidence FOR the efficiency-over-effectiveness critique.
- **Red Bull / Dyson (£750 vacuum) [26:31–27:34]:** Ideas that "did not make sense in advance" — you couldn't pre-rationalize the business case. Evidence FOR post-rationalization over pre-rationalization.
- **The wet trousers / standing in the rain [12:18–13:18]:** Splashed his beige trousers before going on stage; rather than dry the wet patch (hand dryer failed), he stood in the rain to "wet the dry patch." Evidence FOR "the opposite of a good idea is another good idea" and turning a weakness into a strength.
- **The pesometer vs. speedometer [23:30–26:31]:** Same information reframed as minutes-per-10-miles reveals that going 70→85 mph saves ~2 minutes while imposing huge risk. Evidence FOR framing effects — and doubles as a life-safety takeaway.
- **New York fast speech vs. Southern slow speech [16:20]:** Both are politeness — speed respects the listener's scarce time; slowness signals they're worth your time. Evidence FOR "perceptual paradoxes" / opposite-good-ideas.
- **Jeremy Bulmore's "produce an ad even with no media budget" [27:34–28:34]:** The value is in the questions the process forces ("what are we for, who do we serve"). Evidence FOR process-over-output.
- **AI-written student essays [28:34–29:35]:** Essays are worthless as artifacts; their value was the process of writing them. AI leaps to the finished output and bypasses the valuable process. Evidence FOR his warning about AI in creative work.
- **Herodotus / the Persians debating twice — sober and drunk [30:36–31:37]:** Only enact a decision if it appeals to both "sober self and pissed self." His prescription for reintroducing imagination alongside rationality.

## 6. Contrarian claims

- **Speed is not always a benefit.** Orthodoxy: faster is better; optimize to save time. Inversion: "sometimes the time consumed in the pursuit of this activity contributes to the meaning" — train journeys can be too short; people live further from work for a "mental buffer" [11:17–12:18]. Steelman objection: most transactional and industrial contexts genuinely do reward speed; his examples are cherry-picked experience goods (holidays, cruises, courtship), and generalizing "slower is better" would wreck logistics and cost-competitiveness.
- **Efficiency ≠ effectiveness.** Orthodoxy: efficient targeting maximizes ROI. Inversion: efficient (programmatic, impulse) selling captures your least valuable customers and stalls growth [22:29]. Steelman: measurable short-term ROI is what keeps firms solvent; "effectiveness" that can't be measured is indistinguishable from waste to a capital allocator — the very unaccountability he decries cuts both ways.
- **Rational business cases produce worse decisions.** Orthodoxy: require a data-backed business case before spending. Inversion: demanding pre-rationalization filters out exactly the ideas (Red Bull, Dyson) that couldn't be justified in advance [26:31–27:34]. Steelman: for every Red Bull there are thousands of un-rationalizable ideas that failed; the discipline exists because unaccountable "instinct" spending is how money gets wasted at scale.
- **Slow, costly selling can beat cheap, fast selling.** Orthodoxy: minimize cost-per-acquisition. Inversion: handwritten letters and 20-minute demos convert at "10–100x" digital rates because effort is a costly signal [20:27–22:29]. Steelman: these methods don't scale and the quoted multiples are self-serving vendor claims without base rates or margin analysis.
- **AI's efficiency gains in creative work may be net-negative.** Orthodoxy: AI accelerating content creation is pure upside. Inversion: it bypasses the value-generating process, like AI-written essays [28:34–29:35]. Steelman: process value is real for learning, but for many commercial outputs the artifact genuinely is the point, and buyers don't pay for the maker's growth.

## 7. Practical applications

**(a) Marketing / branding**
- Deliberately protect an "explore" budget insulated from quarterly targets, and — equally — build a channel so scout-bee discoveries are actually heard, not ignored [05:10–06:10].
- Use costly signalling: high-effort, slow media (handwritten mail, personal demos) for high-consideration decisions; effort read as commensurate with the decision's scale [20:27–21:29].
- Produce a full advertising campaign even with zero media budget (esp. B2B) to force the "what are we for / who do we serve" questions [27:34].
- Reframe rather than re-engineer: "it's a lot cheaper to change how people see the world" than to change the world [23:30].

**(b) Product & pricing**
- Don't fetishize speed/capacity; ask the open-ended version of the brief (the "Disney" reframe of HS2) [07:10–08:11].
- Reduce uncertainty, not just wait time — the Uber-map principle: give people information/control over the wait [13:18–14:19].
- Segment by conversion cost/speed, not just impulse-readiness; slow, expensive-to-convert customers may hold the real lifetime value [16:20–17:22].
- Beware metrics that let attributable, short-term data define customer value (implicit Goodhart) [16:20].

**(c) Everyday persuasion**
- "Test the opposite": the reverse of a good idea may be another good idea (dry the wet patch vs. wet the dry patch) [11:17, 13:18].
- Institutionalize a "sober and drunk" double debate to let instinct challenge rationality before enacting decisions [30:36].
- Exploit framing: present the same fact two ways to change behaviour (pesometer to curb speeding) [23:30–26:31].
- Match communication tempo to the cultural signal you want (fast = respect scarce time; slow = you're worth my time) [16:20].

## 9. Critical evaluation
The argument is strongest as a diagnosis of institutional incentives: the TfL story and the "unaccountability machine" framing convincingly show that organizations sometimes suppress true findings to preserve model tractability and dodge blame — a genuine, verifiable pathology. The framing-effect and uncertainty-vs-duration points rest on well-established behavioural science. It is weakest where it is most sweeping and least falsifiable: "slower is better" and "everything important takes ages" are survivorship-biased — he cites Red Bull and Dyson but not the far larger pool of un-rationalizable ideas that deservedly failed, and vendor-supplied conversion multiples ("10–100x") are asserted without base rates, margins, or scalability. The bee analogy is an intuition pump, not evidence that firms face extinction dynamics. What would change the picture: controlled or quasi-experimental data on explore/exploit budget allocation and firm growth; base-rate evidence that post-rationalized bets beat pre-rationalized ones after accounting for failures; and cost-per-outcome comparisons for "slow" channels at scale. As it stands, the talk is best read as a persuasive corrective to over-optimization rather than a symmetric decision rule — his own tool, "test the opposite," should be applied to his own thesis.
